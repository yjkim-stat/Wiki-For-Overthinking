## Scope

The gap between a model's stated chain of thought and the computation that produced its answer: unfaithful or post-hoc rationalization, reasoning that is latent rather than written, encoded or steganographic traces, and what a monitor reading the trace can and cannot catch. The question the archive answers is when a visible reasoning trace is evidence about the model, and when it is only text.

## Where the field stands

### 2026

- **INSIDE the Student's Mind: Jointly Modeling Latent Reasoning and Action in LLM Student Simulators** — Fine-tunes student simulators on paired internal-dialogue traces and code edits rather than on actions alone, and measures the result on two axes at once -- how closely generated code matches the distribution of real student submissions, and how well the generated reasoning explains the specific edit that followed.
- **ThinkRetrieve: Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning Traces for Test-Time Scaling** — Injects a retrieved solved problem, with its full worked solution, into the middle of a reasoning model's own thinking trace at each step boundary, using the model's current intermediate answer as the retrieval query.
- **Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs** — Shows that the encrypted reasoning blocks major providers return to clients are interchangeable across sessions, users and models within a provider, and measures four consequences of that interchangeability -- distillation, third-party secret extraction, hazardous-content exposure and monitor-invisible prompt injection -- before responsible disclosure closed it.
- **BDH-CQ: In-Context Learning with Recurrent Latent Reasoning** — Combines in-context learning through evolving recurrent memory with iterative reasoning in a continuous latent workspace that is never decoded into language, reaching 29.5% pass@2 on public ARC-AGI-1 at $0.0007 per task from 150M parameters, and then probes with controlled tasks what the resulting demonstration-bound operators can and cannot do.
- **Think Deep, Speak Once: Relit, A Recursive Latent Implicit Transformer Framework** — Bolts a small trainable recurrent block between a frozen 1.1B language model's body and its output head, so reasoning happens as repeated refinement of two latent vectors rather than as generated tokens.
- **Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Can Be Unreliable in Implicit-Influence Settings** — The first benchmark comparing CoT monitorability under explicit versus implicit influence, finding detection falls 41-46 points when the prompt never instructs the model to hide anything.
- **Does Out-of-Sight Equal Out-of-Mind in CoT Monitorability?** — Asks whether latent CoT destroys monitorability, and finds monitorability depends more on the task and on access to internals than on whether reasoning is explicit or latent.
- **LiLa-WAM: Lightweight Latent Reasoning World-Action Model for Robotic Manipulation** — Builds a 0.5B world-action model for robot manipulation whose future-state prediction and action generation share one compact latent in a single token stream, specifies the task as a direction in visual feature space instead of language, and shows a frozen self-supervised vision encoder beating a four-times-larger pretrained vision-language backbone at the same training budget.
- **LatentGuard: Efficient and Inspectable Latent Reasoning for LLM Safeguards** — Compresses a safety guard's textual rationales into continuous latent states by a staged curriculum, cutting 268 reasoning tokens to 1.60 and latency 8.9-fold, and adds an on-demand decoder that reconstructs a human-readable audit artifact — whose own ablation shows the artifact is anchored far more by the source text than by the latent states it is supposed to inspect.
- **Perception Before Reasoning: Dynamic Latent Reasoning for Video Understanding and Question Answering** — Splits a video model's latent computation into perception latents that always ground the question in visual evidence and reasoning latents allocated only when the question needs inference, and shows that reasoning latents without rationale supervision are worse than no reasoning latents at all.
- **Evading Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Through Model Poisoning** — Shows that supervised fine-tuning can install a triggered backdoor whose visible reasoning stays clean, correct and topically benign while the final answer is attacker-chosen — leaving CoT-only monitors at chance (AUC 0.44-0.55) and recovering detection only when the monitor is shown the answer alongside the trace (0.76-1.00).
- **Latent Thought Credit: Multi-Answer Credit Assignment for Latent Reasoning** — Estimates what a continuous latent thought is worth by freezing the context after it and averaging the rewards of several answers sampled from that fixed context, then credits latent positions with the resulting thought-level advantage and answer positions with the ordinary group-relative one.

### 2025

- **Measuring Chain-of-Thought Monitorability Through Faithfulness and Verbosity** — Argues that faithfulness alone is insufficient for CoT monitoring and adds verbosity — whether the trace lists every factor needed to solve the task — combining the two into a monitorability score, then shows models can look faithful while omitting key factors.
- **Arithmetic Without Algorithms: Language Models Solve Math With a Bag of Heuristics** — Reverse-engineers the arithmetic circuit down to individual neurons and finds it is neither a learned algorithm nor memorization, but an unordered collection of sparse heuristic neurons that each fire on a numerical input pattern and vote for corresponding answers.
- **Demystifying Reasoning Dynamics with Mutual Information: Thinking Tokens are Information Peaks in LLM Reasoning** — Tracks mutual information between each reasoning step's representation and the correct answer, finds it spikes at sparse 'MI peaks' that decode to reflective tokens like 'Wait' and 'Hmm', and shows suppressing exactly those tokens degrades reasoning while suppressing equally many others does not.
- **On Reasoning Strength Planning in Large Reasoning Models** — Shows that a reasoning model decides how long to think before emitting a single reasoning token — the eventual token count is linearly decodable from the question's activations at Spearman 0.84 — and that this plan is carried by one shared direction vector whose magnitude encodes strength and which acts by shifting the logits of the end-of-thinking token.
- **A Implies B: Circuit Analysis in LLMs for Propositional Logical Reasoning** — Uses causal mediation analysis on a minimal propositional logic task to recover a sparse reasoning circuit in Mistral-7B and Gemma-2 up to 27B, and decomposes it into four families of attention heads that execute rule locating, rule moving, fact processing and decision making as sequential steps.
- **Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards Implicitly Incentivizes Correct Reasoning in Base LLMs** — Shows that base models win pass@K on mathematics by producing wrong chains that land on right answers, and that scoring the chain too — CoT-Pass@K — reverses the verdict in RLVR's favour at every K.

### 2024

- **Grokked Transformers are Implicit Reasoners: A Mechanistic Journey to the Edge of Generalization** — Shows that transformers can learn implicit multi-step reasoning over stored knowledge, but only through grokking — extended training far past overfitting — and that whether the resulting circuit generalizes out of distribution depends on the reasoning type, succeeding for comparison and failing for composition.

### 2023

- **Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning** — Measures how much a model's answer actually depends on its stated chain of thought by intervening on the trace — adding mistakes, paraphrasing, truncating — and finds the dependence varies by task and decreases as models get larger.
- **Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting** — Shows that chain-of-thought explanations systematically misrepresent the real reason for a model's answer, by biasing inputs in ways the model never mentions and watching it rationalize the biased answer.

## Core ideas

### credit assignment

Deciding which part of a trajectory earned the reward, and across 31 sources the problem the archive's training literature keeps rediscovering under different names. The complaint is uniformly one of granularity mismatch: a policy is rewarded on whole outputs or whole trajectories while the decisions that produced the result happen at a finer scale, so nothing identifies which retrieval found the key document, which translation choice made the output unfaithful, or which of several changes packed into one code action earned the test pass. The difficulty is worst exactly where the trajectory is longest, since a sparse terminal reward over many turns supplies no supervision at the point of greatest ambiguity. The archive's methods split by where they cut. Some cut by turn, some by trajectory segment type, some by sub-parts of a single action matched across rollouts, and some by token selection -- and their reported contributions are consistent in size, typically 2 to 8 points, against ablations of the terminal signal that are typically 20 to 65. That asymmetry is the corpus's most reliable finding here: finer credit is a refinement of outcome supervision, never a substitute, and every source that removes the terminal reward finds it the largest single loss. Two design constraints follow and are stated independently by several sources. A local term should not be able to flip the sign of the trajectory-level advantage or create an update where the outcome reward is silent -- hence multiplicative rather than additive turn weighting, and gates that stop partial reference agreement overriding a wrong answer. And a criterion for allocating credit is not credited until it has been raced against random allocation at matched cost, because a randomised control routinely captures much of the benefit attributed to the targeted criterion.

Seen in: BiCAA: Bidirectional Credit Assignment for Search-Augmented Agent; EviSD: Evidence-Conditioned Self-Distillation for Search-Augmented Agents; Latent Thought Credit: Multi-Answer Credit Assignment for Latent Reasoning; GradCuit: Credit-Assigned Gradient Flow Enables Robust and Interpretable Test-Time Latent Reasoning.

### overthinking

Producing more reasoning than the problem needs, and across 28 sources the archive's most-attacked inefficiency. The waste is measured rather than asserted: a median of 2.7 thousand emitted tokens against a median 830 needed to reach the correct answer on one benchmark, and 11 to 29 percent token reduction at matched accuracy across three model sizes -- with the savings heavily skewed, reaching 53 percent for the top decile of problems and 81 for the top percentile. Compression sometimes improves accuracy outright (77.5 against 72.5 on one set), which the sources read as redundant steps introducing noise. Three cautions the corpus supplies. Redundancy is diffuse rather than located in an identifiable class of step, so importance criteria agree on what to keep and disagree on what to cut. The same signal that marks productive reasoning marks overthinking on retrieval-style tasks, where amplifying deliberation makes a model derive facts it should retrieve -- one source's candid failure analysis. And a length penalty not gated on correctness is satisfied vacuously, which is why the archive's reward-shaping entry treats conditional length rewards as the only form that converts preserved length into accuracy.

Seen in: Reinforcement Learning with Evolving Rubrics as Rewards for Audio Reasoning; The Tell-Tale Trace: Detecting Reasoning Failures in LLMs Using Chain-of-Thought Dynamics; Fewer Tokens, Smaller Cache: Reward-Coordinated Efficient Reasoning; CAT: Confidence-Adaptive Thinking for Efficient Reasoning of Large Reasoning Models.

### test-time scaling

Spending more computation at inference to get a better answer, and across 28 sources a phrase covering algorithms with different statistical structure, compute accounting and failure modes -- extending one trajectory, sampling completed candidates and reducing them by voting or verification, and searching over unfinished partial states. One source argues the consequence directly: reporting these under a single scalar budget, or reporting accuracy without the inference protocol that produced it, makes results incomparable across papers. The archive's accumulated findings are mostly about where the curve bends. It is not monotone in the sample count, because more candidates strengthen whatever the aggregation rule already does; the selection signal is the binding constraint past a modest budget, and where confidence does not track correctness no verifier-free rule recovers the available headroom (measured at 14 to 17 oracle points on one hard benchmark against 0.8 percent captured). Uniform spending is wasteful in both directions, and adaptive allocation is worth having but hard to learn and hard to audit. And its value is conditional on the instance rather than on the method: budget increases that pay on a feasible instance change nothing on an infeasible one. Two structural observations recur. The budget is almost always spent on the output side -- re-running the model on the same input -- and input-side diversity is a direct competitor for the same calls that has rarely been benchmarked against it at matched compute. And in multimodal settings the usual selection signals fail for a specific reason: neither answer frequency nor verbalised confidence tests whether an answer depends on the image, so a grounded answer and a confident language-prior guess look identical at the selection layer.

Seen in: s1: Simple test-time scaling; It's the Decoding Format, Not the Perturbation: Auditing Consistency-Based Selection for Vision-Language Test-Time Scaling; GradCuit: Credit-Assigned Gradient Flow Enables Robust and Interpretable Test-Time Latent Reasoning; Test-Time Scaling for Safe Text-Guided Image Generation via Intermediate Clean Estimates.

### chain of thought faithfulness

Whether a stated chain of thought is an account of the process that produced the answer, and across 26 sources the archive's central negative result. The measurements are consistent: faithfulness varies substantially across tasks, with models sometimes relying heavily on the trace and sometimes largely ignoring it, and it decreases with model size and capability on most tasks studied -- so the earliest source's own conclusion is a conditional (traces can be faithful if size and task are chosen carefully) rather than an endorsement. Two structural problems recur. The standard test detects unfaithfulness only where an injected cue changes the answer, so it is silent whenever the answer is unchanged, and it intervenes on the prompt rather than on the trace. And the property is not simply desirable: a perfectly faithful agent executes a harmful action whenever its reasoning arrives at one, which makes the usual requirement self-contradictory -- faithful enough to be monitored, robust enough to override its own reasoning. One source measures that tension directly, finding the most faithful model follows tampered safe reasoning in 97.5 percent of cases while rejecting unsafe reasoning in only 12.3, higher faithfulness going with lower safety across seven models, and steering along the two directions trading them against each other by 5 to 9 points against magnitude-matched random vectors moving either by at most 1.1. The archive's operative position is that faithfulness is a relation between the trace and the answer rather than a property of the trace: monitoring works as a consistency check between the two, and a trace can be clean, correct and on-topic while the action taken is arbitrary.

Seen in: Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting; Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning; Does Accuracy Equal Evidence? Reasoning Faithfulness under KV Cache Compression; How Much Does a Reasoning Summary Reveal? An Observability Ladder for Large Language Models.

### adaptive compute allocation

Spending different amounts of inference compute on different inputs, on the premise that a fixed budget wastes effort on easy instances and underserves hard ones. Across 25 sources the case for it is well established and the case for any particular controller is not. Three findings shape how the archive reads such claims. Confounding: a method that changes both the budget and the answer selector cannot say which produced its result, which is why replay protocols holding the candidate bank fixed and varying only the reducer are the informative design -- and under one, selecting by mean token log probability falls from 75.56 to 65.83 percent as the budget grows to eighty while answer plurality rises to 78.33 on the same banks. Controllers are hard to learn and hard to audit: one paper evaluated a learned controller that degenerated to a near-greedy policy under leave-one-dataset-out evaluation and reports it rather than dropping it, and another argues for a transparent controller on the grounds that a fixed budget at least explains itself while a learned policy explains nothing about why a prompt got what it got. That same source reports its own ablations against itself -- removing the entropy signal improves mathematics accuracy, and neutralising confidence improves it while forcing the budget to maximum -- and concludes the controller should be read as a transparent allocation policy rather than an optimised one. And the gain is conditional on the instance rather than on the method: a search planner's yield rises smoothly from 15 to 39 percent between 100 and 5,000 iterations on a nominal instance while a fourfold budget increase changes nothing on an adverse one, because feasibility rather than thoroughness binds. Where the savings are real they are usually large and skewed: a latent-reasoning system cuts 268.56 rationale tokens to 1.60 with an 8.9-fold speedup, and a safety pipeline raises median latency on unsafe prompts from 1.91 to 7.06 seconds while leaving benign prompts at 1.8 to 1.9 regardless of the maximum allowed -- the cost is conditional on detection, not on the budget. Several sources note that adaptive often means whether rather than how much, and that controller overhead against the samples saved is rarely reported.

Seen in: Test-Time Scaling for Safe Text-Guided Image Generation via Intermediate Clean Estimates; LatentGuard: Efficient and Inspectable Latent Reasoning for LLM Safeguards; Interpretable Adaptive Sampling for LLM Test-Time Scaling; Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning LLMs: Inference Regimes, Evaluation, and Reproducibility.

### advantage estimation

Deciding how much better than a baseline a rollout was, which is what a policy update is actually made of. Across 23 sources the corpus's contribution is diagnostic rather than definitional: it shows what goes wrong when the estimate is asymmetric, degenerate or unidentified. Asymmetry is the sharpest case. A regression critic that overestimates success probability gives failed rollouts large negative advantages and successes small positive ones -- measured at -0.597 against +0.227, a ratio of 2.63, growing more skewed over training -- so the update spends most of its magnitude on failures; a distributional critic brings the ratio to 1.02, and the paper notes correctly that batch-wise normalisation is an affine rescaling that cannot remove a relative asymmetry. Degeneracy is the group-relative case: a prompt whose rollouts all share a reward has zero advantage for every member and contributes no gradient, and one source measures a first stage cutting all-wrong groups by 4.95 points precisely to address it. Collapse is a third failure: in latent reasoning, within-thought answer variance exceeds between-thought variance in every setting and training makes it worse (noise-to-signal 1.54 rising to 13.10), so the thought-level advantage is estimating differences that have largely disappeared. Two further findings recur. Weighting is a bias-variance trade rather than a free improvement -- one truncation-order sweep peaks at 4 and degrades beyond it because higher moments cannot be estimated from eight rollouts, and one interpolation sweep runs 45.4, 52.1, 44.8 as its coefficient goes 0.00, 0.25, 1.00. And the corpus's methodological caution applies with unusual force here: margins of 0.75 to 2.6 average points from single runs with no seeds are the norm, and one source's headline is the best average across checkpoints evaluated on the evaluation benchmarks rather than on a held-out criterion.

Seen in: BiCAA: Bidirectional Credit Assignment for Search-Augmented Agent; EviSD: Evidence-Conditioned Self-Distillation for Search-Augmented Agents; Latent Thought Credit: Multi-Answer Credit Assignment for Latent Reasoning; Self-Improving Large Language Models via Progressive Experience Evolution.

### chain of thought

The intermediate tokens a model emits before its answer, and across 23 sources an object the archive is careful to describe rather than to interpret -- one source proposes 'derivational trace' as a neutral stand-in precisely because reading these tokens as reasoning is unsupported by the available evidence and, it argues, actively harmful. What is established is largely formal and negative. Formally, generating intermediate tokens lifts the expressive ceiling of a bounded-depth transformer, with the number of steps tied to a circuit class; one archived construction realises graph traversal in two layers and a linear-step branching measure without layer normalisation, and another shows a sharpness-based bound growing linearly rather than exponentially in the relevant parameter for a parity family. Empirically the trace and the answer are separate information channels: content present in one can be absent from the other in both directions, traces can be swapped between problems or deleted with the answer unchanged, and post-training that improves accuracy need not improve the trace's semantic validity. The corpus's practical positions are that the trace is useful as a consistency check against the answer rather than as an account of computation, that its length is a compute knob with a bend in the curve, and that a large fraction of it is redundant -- with the caveat from a head-to-head comparison that redundancy is diffuse rather than located in any identifiable class of step.

Seen in: STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning; Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models; Attend to Your Own Thoughts: Breaking the Barrier for Post-Training Quantization of Reasoning LLMs through the Lens of 1.58-Bit Quantization; CURV: Enhancing Chart Understanding Through Curriculum Visual Grounded Reasoning.

### pass@k

The probability that at least one of k sampled responses is correct, used across 21 sources as the archive's measure of what a model can reach as opposed to what it reliably produces. Reading it correctly is most of what the corpus has to say. Small k measures sampling efficiency and large k probes the boundary, and the two can move in opposite directions within one training run -- pass@1 rising across steps while coverage at pass@256 falls, and in one distillation study the curve crossing between k=1 and k=1024 in every setting measured. A rising curve is also compatible with within-question stability collapsing, so reporting only coverage hides that the same model now answers inconsistently. Two corrections to naive use are established. Scoring the chain as well as the answer reverses the verdict about reinforcement learning at every k, because base models win plain pass@k partly by producing wrong chains that land on right answers. And for candidate banks produced by adaptive search or dependent procedures the usual estimator is a descriptive summary of what was returned rather than an unbiased probability -- one source states this and notes most papers reporting the metric do not. The archive's practical demand is that a method report pass@k across a range rather than at one budget, since the property worth having is competitiveness with the base model uniformly across k rather than a win at either end.

Seen in: BODHI: Do LLMs Branch Out and Discover Heterogeneous Inferences?; When Correct Solutions Repeat: Rarity-Aware Credit Redistribution for GRPO; Test-Time Scaling in Reasoning LLMs: Inference Regimes, Evaluation, and Reproducibility; PAST: Privileged Adaptation from Complete Student Trajectories for On-Policy Self-Distillation.

### test-time compute

Computation spent at inference rather than training, and across 21 sources the resource the archive's scaling literature is about. Its foundational result here is a trade against parameters: a compute-optimal allocation strategy improves scaling efficiency more than fourfold over a fixed best-of-N baseline, and in a FLOPs-matched evaluation lets a smaller base model outperform a 14 times larger one on problems where it already has non-trivial success rates -- with that last clause being the condition the archive keeps finding attached. The corpus's accumulated qualifications: the same nominal budget buys different things under different regimes, so a single scalar makes results incomparable; more of it is not monotone in accuracy because the aggregation rule is amplified rather than averaged; and its value is conditional on the instance, with budget increases that pay on a feasible instance changing nothing on an infeasible one. One source separates it from a confound worth noting -- an improvement attributed neither to added compute alone nor to information encoded in phrasing.

### monitorability

How much an observer can learn about what a model is doing by reading what it emits, and across 18 sources the safety-adjacent property the archive treats as a relation between task, access and monitor rather than a property of a model. The measured findings: reasoning models are consistently more monitorable than their instruction-tuned counterparts on both axes across all four model pairs tested, with the highest at 78.3 percent average and the largest gaps on the cue where concealment would matter most; difficulty is inversely related to it, with the hardest science benchmark lowest; and scores are similar across all four trace-transition types, which is that source's argument that marking non-switch cases faithful by default throws away most of the available signal. Three limits are stated by the sources themselves. It is almost always evaluated where the prompt directly incentivises concealment, which is not the deployment case. Detection depends on the monitor, so a number bounds that monitor rather than monitorability in general. And where the trace is latent there is no trace to read, so monitoring must fall back on probing or verbalising the latent states, and how much is retained that way is unknown. The archive's own position, from its findings, is that monitoring works as a consistency check between a trace and the answer it accompanies rather than as anomaly detection within the trace.

### residual stream

The additively updated activation vector each transformer layer reads from and writes to, and in this archive the substrate every interpretability method operates on rather than an object studied in itself. What the 17 sources establish about it is mostly negative and methodological. It has no privileged basis: individual neurons are polysemantic and there is no reason to expect features here to align with the neuron basis at all, which is the premise the sparse-autoencoder literature is built on -- and that literature's own numbers show the cost, with automated interpretability scores declining from about 0.30 at layer 0 to 0.15 by layer 5 and reconstruction raising perplexity from 25 to 40 when substituted back. The causal information is often compact: replacing the whole stream at a site has a comparable effect to a low-rank patch, so the relevant subspace is small. And one source names the interpretive gap directly -- aligning explanations to a separately learned codebook answers what a direction means in a proxy space while leaving implicit the rule that actually writes into the stream. Practical scope caveats recur: methods tuned for it do not robustly transfer to intermediate MLP layers, and attention heads are typically untouched.

The Single-Block Theory and Synthetic Validation; Reasoning Errors Have a Region and a Direction in the Residual-Stream Trajectory of LLMs; MI-MIDI: Mechanistic Interpretability of Text-to-MIDI Generation Models via Probing, Lenses and Steering.

### self-correction

A model detecting and repairing its own error, and across 17 sources a capability the archive can measure the behaviour of more easily than the benefit. The central finding is a dissociation: how often a model announces a correction is anti-correlated with whether the corrections help. Against two prior supervised methods on a shared backbone, a step-level preference method scores higher on both mathematics benchmarks while self-correcting at 28.8 and 23.9 percent against 45.3 and 46.5 and recalling fewer errors, and two of its own five backbones lower their correction rate after training while improving. The diagnosis offered is behaviour collapse under supervised training, which biases a model toward following correction templates rather than selectively revising real errors. Two measurement problems follow. The behavioural metrics are defined by matching a list of signal phrases, so a model that corrects without using one is invisible to the measurement, and the training data's preferred continuations are defined by the same convention. And the construct has almost no clean measurement window: models weak enough to have headroom lack the signal, models with the signal lack the headroom, so the design space where self-correction could be measured cleanly is nearly empty -- one source's calibration-floor argument finds only one marginally viable cell with negligible held-out gain. Where the archive finds it working is as a trained selectivity rather than a prompted behaviour, with the preference formed between a repair and the continuation that would have followed had the error stood, so the signal exists only where an error does.

Seen in: How Much Does a Reasoning Summary Reveal? An Observability Ladder for Large Language Models; The Tell-Tale Trace: Detecting Reasoning Failures in LLMs Using Chain-of-Thought Dynamics; The Calibration Floor: Format Repair Can Masquerade as Self-Correction at Small-to-Mid Scale; Refining Over Resampling: Test-Time Self-Correction for LLM Reasoning.

## Reading path

**Start here** — the anchor papers for this topic:

1. 2305.04388
1. 2307.13702

**Then, in order of relevance:**

1. **Reasoning Traces Shape Outputs but Models Won&apos;t Say So** (2026)
   - Injects synthetic reasoning into a model's trace, shows the injection changes the answer, then shows the model refuses to admit it and fabricates an unrelated explanation instead.
   - <https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.1986>
2. **Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Can Be Unreliable in Implicit-Influence Settings** (2026)
   - The first benchmark comparing CoT monitorability under explicit versus implicit influence, finding detection falls 41-46 points when the prompt never instructs the model to hide anything.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04735>
3. **Does Out-of-Sight Equal Out-of-Mind in CoT Monitorability?** (2026)
   - Asks whether latent CoT destroys monitorability, and finds monitorability depends more on the task and on access to internals than on whether reasoning is explicit or latent.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04928>
4. **Evading Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Through Model Poisoning** (2026)
   - Shows that supervised fine-tuning can install a triggered backdoor whose visible reasoning stays clean, correct and topically benign while the final answer is attacker-chosen — leaving CoT-only monitors at chance (AUC 0.44-0.55) and recovering detection only when the monitor is shown the answer alongside the trace (0.76-1.00).
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02820>
5. **INSIDE the Student's Mind: Jointly Modeling Latent Reasoning and Action in LLM Student Simulators** (2026)
   - Fine-tunes student simulators on paired internal-dialogue traces and code edits rather than on actions alone, and measures the result on two axes at once -- how closely generated code matches the distribution of real student submissions, and how well the generated reasoning explains the specific edit that followed.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10492>
6. **ThinkRetrieve: Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning Traces for Test-Time Scaling** (2026)
   - Injects a retrieved solved problem, with its full worked solution, into the middle of a reasoning model's own thinking trace at each step boundary, using the model's current intermediate answer as the retrieval query.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10928>
7. **Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs** (2026)
   - Shows that the encrypted reasoning blocks major providers return to clients are interchangeable across sessions, users and models within a provider, and measures four consequences of that interchangeability -- distillation, third-party secret extraction, hazardous-content exposure and monitor-invisible prompt injection -- before responsible disclosure closed it.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09867>
8. **BDH-CQ: In-Context Learning with Recurrent Latent Reasoning** (2026)
   - Combines in-context learning through evolving recurrent memory with iterative reasoning in a continuous latent workspace that is never decoded into language, reaching 29.5% pass@2 on public ARC-AGI-1 at $0.0007 per task from 150M parameters, and then probes with controlled tasks what the resulting demonstration-bound operators can and cannot do.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09888>
9. **LiLa-WAM: Lightweight Latent Reasoning World-Action Model for Robotic Manipulation** (2026)
   - Builds a 0.5B world-action model for robot manipulation whose future-state prediction and action generation share one compact latent in a single token stream, specifies the task as a direction in visual feature space instead of language, and shows a frozen self-supervised vision encoder beating a four-times-larger pretrained vision-language backbone at the same training budget.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03701>
10. **LatentGuard: Efficient and Inspectable Latent Reasoning for LLM Safeguards** (2026)
   - Compresses a safety guard's textual rationales into continuous latent states by a staged curriculum, cutting 268 reasoning tokens to 1.60 and latency 8.9-fold, and adds an on-demand decoder that reconstructs a human-readable audit artifact — whose own ablation shows the artifact is anchored far more by the source text than by the latent states it is supposed to inspect.
   - <https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03838>

## Open problems

Drawn from the limitations each paper states about itself, so this is what the field admits it cannot do yet.

- **INSIDE the Student's Mind: Jointly Modeling Latent Reasoning and Action in LLM Student Simulators** — Stated, and the first is the deepest: the internal dialogue is reconstructed rather than observed, generated by a teacher model through retrospective inference, so it approximates rather than records the student's cognition. The authors add the specific reason this is more than a caveat -- models trained to produce expert-like reasoning may be poor at reconstructing novice reasoning even in a reconstruction setting, so the traces may be more coherent and better structured than what real students exhibit -- and name think-aloud studies and retrospective verbalisation as the validation that has not been done. The two test splits differ not only in whether problems were seen but in their underlying student pass-rate distributions, which the authors say directly affects how the results should be read. And alignment at about 58 percent means a majority of generated claims explain the observed edits, with the remainder a gap, especially against teacher-generated traces which align near-perfectly. Not stated but worth noticing: the reasoning-quality metric is alignment with an edit, which rewards a trace that predicts what happened without establishing that it is what the student was thinking -- the same trace-versus-cause distinction the archive holds elsewhere, here unavoidable because there is no ground-truth cognition to compare against. The unseen-split gaps are within roughly one standard error, and the whole evaluation is one programming-education setting.
- **ThinkRetrieve: Retrieval-Augmented Reasoning Traces for Test-Time Scaling** — No limitations section in the material read. What a reader should weigh first is the retrieval encoder: the related-work section cites a result that structurally faithful retrieval over mathematics is hard with off-the-shelf encoders, and the method then uses an off-the-shelf E5-Large, with the encoder choice relegated to an ablation. Second, decontamination is by cosine similarity at 0.90 and the audit reports maximum retained similarities of 0.898 and 0.891 -- immediately below the cut, with mean retained similarities of 0.866 and 0.845, which the paper attributes to the structural density of synthetic math corpora rather than leakage, and defends with an answer-distinct retrieval control rather than with a lower threshold. Third, the headline gains sit on AIME 2025, which is 30 problems, so +13.4 is four problems even at three seeds. Fourth, three of the four benchmarks share the same NuminaMath bank and the fourth uses its own training split, so 'adapts to different example banks' rests on one swap, and nothing here tests a domain where a bank of solved problems does not exist. Finally, the entropy reduction is offered as the explanation of the gain but is measured alongside it rather than manipulated, so it is a correlate of the improvement and not shown to cause it.
- **Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs** — Stated: the evaluation is bounded to specific API versions and reasoning models available from three providers in early July 2026, and the providers' cryptographic implementations are proprietary and subject to unannounced change, so the attacks' efficacy will differ. The extraction depends on a decoder model's stochastic generation and there is no ground-truth plaintext to check against, so while token-count comparisons suggest high fidelity the authors cannot verify every extracted token; occasional refusals and noise are handled by an optional reconciliation over up to three non-refusal samples. The scan of public traces is a targeted demonstration rather than an exhaustive audit, and the authors expect private and production traces to be more affected than public ones, which is an assumption rather than a measurement. Not stated but worth noticing: the distillation result rests on 30 problems and one style metric -- n-gram overlap with the first 100 tokens of an answer -- which measures surface convergence rather than transferred capability, and the paper makes no claim that a model trained on such traces performs better; the effect appears in one of two open models tested, so how general it is remains open. The privacy figures depend on an LLM judge for the leakage label with no reported human validation of that classifier's precision, and 'distinct artifact' counts across a scraped corpus cannot be de-duplicated against unknown overlap between repositories.
- **BDH-CQ: In-Context Learning with Recurrent Latent Reasoning** — Stated, and unusually carefully. The ConceptARC replication rules out a combined identifier-and-batch confound but explicitly does not make ConceptARC a fresh benchmark, does not rule out exposure through training or checkpoint selection, and does not isolate the two interventions factorially. Ten tasks per concept family is called a profile rather than a reliable ranking, with the Wilson intervals for 9/10 and 2/10 overlapping broadly. And nominal pass@2 should not be read as two independently sampled attempts, since all 75 single-candidate records in the opaque replication were already correct at rank one. Not stated but worth noticing: the headline is a cost-efficiency claim and the accuracy is 29.5% on a benchmark where far more expensive systems score much higher, so the Pareto framing is doing real work and the comparison to $1.48 and $1.76 per task is against systems whose cost includes puzzle-specific optimisation the authors' does not need -- an architecture difference as much as an efficiency one. The controlled probes are generated by the authors after freezing the model, which is the right protocol, but they are ARC-like synthetic families of their own construction, so the extrapolation and composition conclusions are about those constructions. Nothing here is tested outside visual grid transformations, and the paper's own closing section lists language and mathematical reasoning as future work.
- **Think Deep, Speak Once: Relit, A Recursive Latent Implicit Transformer Framework** — No limitations section, and the central comparison does not support the claim it is used for. ReLIT is **trained on each dataset's training split** -- 5,000 ProofWriter examples, 5,000 RuleTaker, 800 HELP, 2,000 TaxiNLI -- while every LLM number in the same table is taken from GLoRE under few-shot prompting. The paper says as much when introducing the setup and then tabulates the two side by side, so 'matching or outperforming significantly larger models' is a comparison between a task-specific trained head and models that saw no training data. On the two tasks where the training set is large and the label space is a verdict, ReLIT reaches 97-99 percent; on the three where it is small or the task is linguistic, it does not. Second, the repeated inference that stability implies correctness is not supported: 'the outputs remain consistent after this stabilization phase' and 'high confidence in the final supervision loops proves that the model is not hallucinating' conflate convergence with being right, and this archive holds a direct counter-case in work showing intermediate answers stabilize regardless of whether the answer is ultimately correct. Third, one backbone, one scale, no seeds or variance anywhere, and no ablation separating the residual-refinement novelty from deep supervision, adaptive halting or the frozen head. Finally, the efficiency argument is asserted rather than measured -- no latency, token or FLOP comparison against chain-of-thought appears, though avoiding token generation is the paper's motivation.
- **Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Can Be Unreliable in Implicit-Influence Settings** — The 41-46 point drop is reported for two of four settings, so the effect is not uniform across task formats and the other two are not characterized in the abstract. Nudges are constructed, and their strength relative to real deployment biases is unknown. Detection depends on the monitor used, so the numbers bound this monitor rather than monitorability in general.
- **Does Out-of-Sight Equal Out-of-Mind in CoT Monitorability?** — No quantitative results in the abstract. Hint-reliance is one proxy target, and a negative result about reasoning mode on this target need not transfer to other monitored behaviours. Models and probe architectures are not named. The finding is comparative and does not establish an absolute level of monitorability for any mode.
- **LiLa-WAM: Lightweight Latent Reasoning World-Action Model for Robotic Manipulation** — No limitations section appears in the main text. What a reader should weigh: the headline comparison is close enough that the ordering depends on which column is read — the method leads on clean success and trails on randomized against the strongest similar-size baseline — and no seeds or variance are reported anywhere, on any table. The task token is computed offline from that task's own demonstrations, so specifying a genuinely new task requires demonstrations of it, which is a weaker form of instruction-following than the language conditioning it replaces and is not framed as a trade-off in the text. The ablations run on a 10-task subset while the headline uses 50, and the backbone comparison substitutes both the encoder and the task-conditioning at once, so the 9-point gap is not attributable to the backbone alone. The causal probe reads similarity through the training-time decoder rather than through anything used at inference, and the real-robot evaluation is not quantified in the portion of the paper reporting simulation results.
- **LatentGuard: Efficient and Inspectable Latent Reasoning for LLM Safeguards** — The paper has no limitations section, and one of its own framings should be read as the central caveat: the auxiliary decoder is explicitly not intended to recover the guard's internal reasoning process, only to produce artifacts that make decisions easier to review. Given that the ablation attributes most of the artifact's utility to source-text conditioning, a reader should treat the audit output as a plausible post-hoc account conditioned on the same input a human could read, not as a window into the latent computation — and nothing here tests whether the artifact and the verdict can disagree, which is the check that would distinguish the two. Further: the audit-utility metric is scored by a model judge, validated against human annotation on 386 samples by an annotator using the same criteria as that judge, so the two are not independent. All models are initialized from one reasoning-guard family and trained on one corpus, so the improvement is measured against the checkpoint the method starts from rather than against independently trained baselines. And the latency advantage holds against reasoning guards while two classification guards remain faster, so the efficiency claim is relative to the family being replaced.
- **Perception Before Reasoning: Dynamic Latent Reasoning for Video Understanding and Question Answering** — The paper has no limitations section. What a reader should weigh: the latent budgets are fixed at inference, so 'adaptive' means whether to reason rather than how much, and the routing is the only quantity the reinforcement stage adjusts. The training supervision — evidence boxes and rationales alike — is generated by one large model and filtered by another, so both the grounding targets and the distillation teacher are model outputs rather than human annotation, and the paper's own quality control is a human audit deferred to an appendix. No seeds or variance are reported for any table. And the interpretability claim rests on attention heatmaps concentrating on diagnostic cues, which the paper offers as a complementary non-textual form of explanation — a weak substitute, since attention concentration shows where the model looked and not that the answer depended on it.
- **Evading Chain-of-Thought Monitoring Through Model Poisoning** — The paper states its bounds directly: three open-weight reasoning models between 3.8B and 12B parameters, with every backdoor installed through supervised fine-tuning, so whether the same behaviour and mechanism arise at larger scale or through reinforcement learning and preference optimization is open. It also names the cost of its own recommendation — consistency monitoring requires recovering the conclusion implied by a trace, which is difficult in open-ended agentic settings where there may be no parseable conclusion to compare against. Two things a reader should add. The threat model grants the adversary full control of fine-tuning, which is the strongest position available and makes this an upper bound on the attack rather than a claim about likelihood; the authors note the poisoned models also stand in for the weaker case of a model inadvertently learning misaligned behaviour during training. And the one defence tested, a KL penalty toward the base distribution, reduces attack success from 94.0% to 79.3% — which the paper correctly describes as a cost to the attacker rather than a defence, leaving the defensive side essentially unexplored.
- **Latent Thought Credit: Multi-Answer Credit Assignment for Latent Reasoning** — The paper states its scope in the conclusion rather than a limitations section: two Qwen2.5-Instruct sizes, mathematics and STEM multiple choice with verifiable or easily normalized rewards, and fixed K and M, with noisier open-ended rewards, other model families, and the interpretability of the continuous thoughts left as future work. Two things a reader should add. First, the diagnostics undercut the mechanism more than the paper says: after training, thought-level utilities are so close together that ordering them is barely better than chance, which means the thought-level advantage — the paper's central signal — is estimating differences that have largely collapsed. The paper reads the small regret as reassurance, but the honest statement is that the credit signal survives because choosing wrong costs little, not because it chooses well. Second, the tuning surface is broad and task-dependent: the matching strength, the top-k support size (non-monotonic, with the highest-peak setting finishing a full point below the best final one) and the K-M split each have a different optimum per dataset, and all of them are read off the evaluation benchmarks. Gains over HRPO are around 0.9 to 1.2 average points with no seeds or variance reported.
