Your Reasoning Benchmark May Not Test Reasoning: Revealing Perception Bottleneck in Abstract Reasoning Benchmarks

Xinhe Wang 0001, Jin Huang, Xingjian Zhang 0002, Tianhao Wang et al. · ACL · 2026-01-01 · dblp

<https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.826>

Separates perception from reasoning in ARC-style benchmarks with a two-stage pipeline, and finds about 80% of vision-language model failures are perception errors, not reasoning errors.

Problem. ARC and ARC-AGI are read as probes of core fluid reasoning, and the gap between human and frontier vision-language model performance is commonly attributed to deficient machine reasoning. That attribution is untested: the tasks require seeing the grids before reasoning about them, so a perception failure is indistinguishable from a reasoning failure under end-to-end scoring.

Contributions.

  • The hypothesis that the ARC-style human-model gap is primarily perceptual rather than inductive
  • A two-stage pipeline that isolates reasoning from perception while preventing cross-image inductive leakage
  • Evidence across Mini-ARC, ACRE and Bongard-LOGO that perception dominates the gap
  • A manual trace analysis attributing approximately 80% of failures to perception errors
  • The conclusion that ARC-style benchmarks conflate perception with reasoning

Method. A two-stage pipeline explicitly separates the two. In the perception stage each image is independently converted into a natural-language description; in the reasoning stage a model induces and applies rules using only those descriptions. Converting each image independently is essential — it prevents leakage of cross-image inductive signal into the perception stage, so the reasoning stage is genuinely isolated from perception. Two-stage results are compared against standard end-to-end one-stage evaluation, and reasoning traces are manually inspected.

Results. Across Mini-ARC, ACRE and Bongard-LOGO, perception capability is the dominant factor behind the observed performance gap. Manual inspection of VLM reasoning traces finds approximately 80% of model failures stem from perception errors. The authors conclude ARC-style benchmarks conflate perceptual and reasoning challenges and that observed gaps may overstate deficiencies in machine reasoning.

Limitations. Three ARC-style datasets, so the conclusion is about this benchmark family. The perception stage's output is a natural-language description, so reasoning is evaluated over a lossy re-encoding — an upper bound on reasoning given perfect description is not the same as reasoning over the image. The 80% figure comes from manual inspection whose protocol, sample size and agreement are not given in the abstract. Models are not named.

Relevance. Directly attacks the interpretation of the benchmark family most often cited as evidence about machine reasoning. If 80% of failures are perceptual, then ARC-AGI scores are largely a vision measurement, and progress reported on them is not progress on reasoning. The methodological contribution is the leakage control: converting each image independently is what makes the isolation valid, and without it the perception stage could smuggle the induction. This is the fourth paper in one drain arguing a reasoning benchmark measures something else — with SMART on aggregation, VisAidMath on visual aids, CoRE on code execution — which is enough co-occurrence to treat construct validity as the drain's dominant theme rather than four separate findings.

Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems

Karl Cobbe, Vineet Kosaraju, Mohammad Bavarian, Mark Chen et al. · 2021-10-27 · seed

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168>

Introduces GSM8K, 8.5K grade-school math word problems, and shows that training a verifier to rank many sampled solutions beats finetuning the generator directly.

Problem. Language models matched human performance on many tasks yet failed at robust multi-step mathematical reasoning, and the abstract notes that even the largest transformers of the time scored poorly on a problem distribution that is conceptually simple. There was no diagnostic dataset isolating that failure.

Contributions.

  • GSM8K: 8.5K high-quality, linguistically diverse grade school math word problems, built to diagnose multi-step reasoning failure.
  • The finding that model size alone does not solve this distribution.
  • Training verifiers to judge the correctness of completions, and using them at test time to rank many sampled candidates.
  • Evidence that verification scales better with additional data than a finetuning baseline.

Method. Many candidate solutions are sampled at test time and a separately trained verifier judges the correctness of each; the highest-ranked candidate is returned. This is contrasted against finetuning the generator to produce the answer directly. The abstract does not give the verifier's architecture, training data construction, or sample count.

Results. The abstract states that verification significantly improves performance on GSM8K and that it scales more effectively with increased data than the finetuning baseline, but gives no accuracy figures, model sizes or sample counts. Summarized from the abstract alone, so the figures below are only those the abstract states; the paper's full evaluation is not represented here.

Limitations. Not discussed in the abstract. What a reader of this archive should note is downstream rather than internal: GSM8K has since become a saturated benchmark, and later archived work uses it as the easy rung of a suite and raises contamination concerns about publicly available math benchmarks in general.

Relevance. The origin of the benchmark that nearly every archived paper reports on, which makes it the reference point for what those numbers mean. Two things matter for this topic. First, its framing: the dataset was built as a diagnostic for a specific failure, not as a leaderboard, and it was designed around problems whose conceptual simplicity makes a low score informative. Second, its fate: a benchmark introduced because large models could not solve it is now the easy end of every suite in this archive, so it is a concrete case study in benchmark saturation and in why a claim resting on GSM8K gains needs a harder companion set. This is also the first paper filed under this topic.

SafeSceneReason: A Multimodal Reasoning Benchmark Connecting Industrial Hazards with Accident Knowledge

Yuanchi Zhu, Kang An, Tengyue Wang, Zhongyu Yang et al. · 2026-08-10 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09230>

Builds an industrial-safety reasoning benchmark from two pipelines -- program execution over safety scene graphs, and evidence graphs extracted from real accident-investigation reports -- and shows that general multimodal capability does not transfer to it while a 9B model fine-tuned on its chain-of-thought split matches frontier systems.

Problem. Industrial-safety understanding is not a recognition task: a worker can be wearing the required protective equipment and still be standing behind a reversing forklift, and a technician beside a stopped machine can be injured if it restarts because its energy source was not isolated. Judging these requires the whole scene, the task being performed, and how workers, equipment and safeguards interact, then connecting that to accident knowledge and to a preventive action. Existing safety datasets evaluate local or isolated cues -- protective-equipment presence, unsafe actions, individual violations -- and give little supervision for the progression from scene understanding through exposure assessment and accident-mechanism reasoning to intervention.

Contributions.

  • SafeSceneReason: 110,581 verified scene-centric and 13,114 refined report-centric multimodal question-answer pairs spanning eight reasoning categories from perception through compliance and causal analysis to mitigation, with retained evidence, reasoning paths, provenance and verification records.
  • A scene-centric construction pipeline in which answers are produced by program execution over an executable safety scene graph of objects, relations and rules, so ground truth is deterministic and replayable rather than authored by a model.
  • A report-centric pipeline that grounds questions in real accident investigations from OSHA, NIOSH FACE and the Chemical Safety Board using evidence graphs with explicit information boundaries and iterative verification, including multi-figure cases.
  • Evidence that general multimodal capability does not transfer to safety reasoning -- a 51.3-point spread among untuned models -- and that no frontier model dominates all eight categories, with the two strongest splitting perception-linked against cross-evidence and decision-oriented tasks.
  • A demonstration that chain-of-thought supervision outperforms direct-answer supervision by a wide margin on the same data (89.0% against 75.1% micro), concentrated in comparative, quantitative and compliance reasoning, while leaving hazard identification, evidence synthesis and mitigation as the residual gap.

Method. Two complementary construction pipelines. The scene-centric pipeline converts annotated workplace images into executable safety scene graphs over objects, relations and safety rules, and derives answers by program execution, so the answer is deterministic and replayable rather than model-written. The report-centric pipeline starts from more than 80,000 publicly available accident reports from OSHA, NIOSH FACE and the Chemical Safety Board, screens for reports whose figures, captions and contextual text are informative enough for multimodal construction, parses them with MinerU into text, figures, captions and page-level structure, and builds questions on evidence graphs with explicit information boundaries, multi-step reasoning paths and iterative verification, retaining provenance and verification records. The result is 110,581 verified scene-centric and 13,114 refined report-centric question-answer pairs across eight reasoning categories: quantitative, spatial/structural, causal, comparative, evidence synthesis, hazard identification, compliance assessment, and mitigation/decision making. Evaluation uses a 1,000-example test set balanced across answer positions, with every system under the same prompt and deterministic decoding (temperature 0, top-p 1), an answer counted correct only when the parser extracts exactly one matching option and ambiguous or unparseable outputs scored incorrect, reported as both micro accuracy and category-macro accuracy. Two safety-domain adaptations of Qwen3.5-9B are trained by LoRA SFT with the visual encoder and multimodal aligner frozen, rank-16 adapters (alpha 32, dropout 0.05) on the language model, two epochs in bfloat16 at sequence length 4,096, effective batch 64, AdamW at 1e-4, selecting the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint: one on answer-only supervision (8,810 train examples) and one on chain-of-thought supervision (8,783). Decontamination removes records matching the test set by sample ID, source-document ID, normalized question or image path, and splits are grouped by source document.

Results. The spread among untuned models is the headline: 88.7% micro for GPT-5.6-Sol and 87.9% for GPT-5.5, a second tier of 80.3% and 78.7% for Gemini-3.1-Pro and Gemini-3.5-Flash, then 74.1% for Claude-Fable-5 and 70.7% for Kimi-K2.6, against 40.9% for Qwen3.6-27B and 37.4% for Qwen3.5-9B -- a 51.3-point spread. No proprietary model dominates every category: GPT-5.6-Sol leads quantitative (87.8%), spatial/structural (86.6%), causal (93.3%) and hazard identification (89.3%), while GPT-5.5 leads comparative (86.1%), evidence synthesis (93.1%), compliance (94.6%) and mitigation (96.3%). Safety-domain supervision closes almost all of the open-model gap: answer-only LoRA SFT lifts Qwen3.5-9B from 37.4% to 75.1% micro, and chain-of-thought SFT reaches 89.0% micro and 88.7% macro -- gains of 51.6 and 46.8 points, and the highest micro accuracy of any configuration tested, slightly above GPT-5.6-Sol's 88.7%, though GPT-5.5 keeps the best macro at 89.5% so the fine-tuned model is less uniform across categories. The categories that improve most under CoT supervision are comparative reasoning (+67.6 points), quantitative (+63.4) and compliance (+60.8), and the fine-tuned model's weakest remaining categories are hazard identification (82.1%), evidence synthesis (85.1%) and mitigation (85.2%) -- so it stays below the strongest proprietary systems exactly where broad domain knowledge and visual grounding matter rather than procedure. Two diagnostic findings are reported carefully. Base Qwen3.5-9B scores 52.4% when option A is correct and only 31.6-33.6% for the other positions on a position-balanced test set, which is a response-format shortcut rather than reasoning, and CoT supervision reduces composition errors without fully fixing calibration. And domain difficulty does not track how much data a domain has: materials/structures and machinery/guarding each hold 49 questions but Kimi-K2.6 scores 73.5% and 61.2%, while chemical toxicology is one of the largest domains at 80 questions and among the hardest at 43.8%, against 81.2% on agriculture. The authors flag their own outliers, noting that mining at 91.7% (n=12) and noise/hearing at 83.3% (n=6) carry wide standard errors and should not be read as easy domains. All numbers are software evaluations; local inference used eight A100 80GB GPUs and proprietary models were accessed by API under the same protocol.

Limitations. Stated: the benchmark is described as a focused step rather than a representation of the occupational-safety lifecycle, with coverage bounded by the available workplace annotations, the selected accident-report sources and the safety concepts that traceable visual and textual evidence can support; small domains carry wide intervals that the authors explicitly warn against over-reading; and they conclude that high aggregate accuracy is insufficient for safety-critical use given the persistent failures in evidence integration, calibration and intervention selection. Not stated but worth noticing: the headline that a 9B model matches frontier systems is a within-distribution result -- it is fine-tuned on this corpus and evaluated on this corpus's test split, so it measures how learnable the benchmark's question templates are, not safety reasoning transfer, and the paper offers no held-out evaluation on a different safety resource. The evaluation is entirely multiple choice scored by exact option match, which cannot distinguish a correct answer reached from the wrong evidence, and the mitigation category -- arguably the one that matters for deployment -- rests on 27 questions. Scene-centric answers are deterministic by construction because they are program outputs over a scene graph, so their correctness inherits whatever the underlying annotations and rule encodings assume, and the report-centric half depends on MinerU's parsing of figures and captions from PDFs. Finally the domain-difficulty analysis uses a historical Kimi-K2.6 replay at 65.0% overall rather than the benchmark table, so the domain conclusions rest on one mid-tier model.

Relevance. Three things here are useful independently of the safety domain. First, it is a clean demonstration that a benchmark can separate models that aggregate benchmarks do not: a 51.3-point spread among untuned models, and two frontier systems that split the eight categories cleanly between them -- one ahead on quantitative, spatial, causal and hazard identification, the other on comparative, evidence synthesis, compliance and mitigation -- so a single overall number would have ranked them as near-equivalent and hidden a real difference in kind. That is an argument for category-macro reporting alongside micro, which this paper does and which its own results justify, since the ordering of its top three configurations changes between the two. Second, it catches a response-format shortcut with a control worth copying: the test set is balanced across answer positions, and base Qwen3.5-9B scores 52.4% when the correct option is A against 31.6-33.6% elsewhere. On an unbalanced set that model would have looked meaningfully better than chance at something it was not doing. Third, the domain analysis argues explicitly that difficulty is not determined by representation frequency -- chemical toxicology is one of the largest domains and among the hardest, while two domains of identical size differ by 12 points -- and the authors flag their own small-n outliers rather than reporting them as findings, which is the right handling. The result to treat with most care is the headline that a 9B model with chain-of-thought SFT reaches the highest micro accuracy of any system tested. It is fine-tuned on this corpus and evaluated on this corpus's held-out split, with decontamination by sample, document, question text and image path, so it establishes that the benchmark's question forms are learnable at 9B rather than that safety reasoning transferred. The more interesting comparison inside that result is CoT supervision against answer-only supervision on identical data, 89.0% against 75.1%, with gains concentrated in comparative and quantitative reasoning and the residual gap concentrated in hazard identification, evidence synthesis and mitigation -- consistent with the reading that explicit intermediate supervision buys procedure and not knowledge.

Calibrating Post-Training Feature Shifts for LLM Data Contamination Detection

Zhen Yang, Mengqi Wang, Gengda Zhao, Mo Zhou et al. · 2026-08-11 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10462>

Calibrates feature-based data-contamination detectors against the systematic feature shifts that post-training introduces, by measuring how controlled prompt variants move scores on known non-members and then correcting only the recurring shift directions, with the gains concentrated at the low-false-positive operating point rather than in AUC.

Problem. Data contamination detection asks whether a given text was in a model's pre-training corpus, and the strongest current methods are feature-based: derive membership features from the input text and the model's output, then train a classifier to separate members from non-members. That paradigm inherits a vulnerability. Modern models are post-trained by instruction tuning, preference optimization and reasoning-oriented training, all of which change the style, length, structure and content of generated output, which shifts the resulting membership features and reduces the separation between members and non-members. Detectors built before that shift degrade against models that have undergone it.

Contributions.

  • A calibration framework applicable to existing feature-based contamination detectors rather than a replacement detector, so the post-training shift is corrected without retraining or redesigning the detector.
  • Multi-View Shift Detection: evaluating controlled prompt variants on known non-members, prioritising views by the false-positive pressure they induce, and taking a cross-view consensus to isolate recurring shift directions from view-specific noise.
  • Bounded Feature Correction: adjusting only the components aligned with detected shifts, with score-sensitive weights and an adaptively selected correction extent below full, on the argument that over-correction removes detection signal along with shift.
  • An ablation set showing that the consensus step carries the most AUC and single-view restriction costs the most at the low-false-positive operating point, with the complete method ahead in 23 or 24 of 24 settings on every comparison.
  • An explicit statement that the corrected shifts are identified by association with elevated non-member scores rather than shown to be caused by post-training.

Method. CalibDCD is a calibration wrapper rather than a new detector, applied on top of existing feature-based methods in the standard black-box setting where the detector may query the model and read its outputs but has no access to parameters or internals. It has two stages. Multi-View Shift Detection evaluates a set of controlled prompt variants -- eight universal views plus two model-specific ones -- on texts known to be non-members, scores each view by the false-positive pressure it produces, prioritises the most informative, and takes a cross-view consensus so that recurring shift directions are retained while view-specific idiosyncrasies are dropped. Bounded Feature Correction then adjusts only the feature components aligned with those detected directions, with score-sensitive rather than binary weights and a correction extent below full so that useful detection information is preserved; the correction strength is selected adaptively per setting rather than fixed. Evaluation covers two feature-based detectors across four contamination benchmarks and three target model families, reporting both AUC and true positive rate at 5 percent false positive rate, with ablations that replace view prioritisation with random selection, restrict to the single best view, remove the consensus step, replace the weighting with binary, and force full correction on the eighteen settings where the adaptive rule chose less.

Results. The improvement is real but its size depends entirely on which metric is read, and the two diverge sharply. AUC gains are small: averaged by model, 2.4 percent for Qwen, 1.7 for Llama, 2.3 for DeepSeek, with the largest benchmark-level average of 4.0 on BookMIA, and per-cell movements typically of one to three points (the strongest detector goes from 88.8 to 91.7 on one cell, 78.5 to 84.0 on another). At the 5 percent false-positive operating point the same corrections are worth several times more: the stronger detector moves from 53.6 to 68.6 on BookTection with Qwen, 45.3 to 58.0 with DeepSeek, 41.5 to 49.8 on BookMIA, and 74.8 to 81.6 on WikiMIA, while the weaker detector roughly doubles on several cells (15.3 to 26.0, 20.2 to 32.2, 6.6 to 15.5). Since AUC integrates over all thresholds and contamination detection is used at a low false-positive threshold, the second set is the operationally relevant one and the first understates the method. Every ablation degrades both metrics in essentially every setting, and the ordering is informative: removing cross-view consensus costs the most AUC (1.3 points, worse in all 24 settings), while restricting to the single best view costs the most at the operating point (4.2 points of TPR, worse in all 24). Replacing informed view prioritisation with three random views costs only 0.6 AUC and 3.1 TPR, so most of the benefit comes from combining several views at all rather than from choosing them well. Forcing full correction where the adaptive rule chose partial costs 0.9 AUC and 2.6 TPR across those eighteen settings, confirming that over-correcting removes signal along with shift. The weaker of the two detectors starts near chance on several cells (53.5, 54.3, 57.0 AUC), so its improvements are improvements on a detector that was barely discriminating.

Limitations. Stated, and the most important one is a disclaimer about the paper's own framing: the method corrects score-increasing feature shifts observed on known non-members, and while post-training motivates the study, those shifts may equally reflect dataset artifacts, decoding behaviour or the feature extractor -- so what is identified is a shift associated with higher non-member scores during calibration, not a shift proved to be caused by post-training. The method also requires a pool of known non-members held outside the supervised classifier's training, which temporal benchmarks and post-cutoff data supply but which is hard to obtain when a model's cutoff is unknown or no trusted non-member pool exists; and the calibration examples remain in the reporting pool under their protocol. Not stated but worth noticing: the AUC improvements are one to four points, which on four benchmarks and three model families with no confidence intervals or repeated runs reported is a thin margin, and the large TPR movements are measured at a single operating point on benchmarks whose positive sets are small. The three target models are described only by family, so what specific post-training each underwent -- the variable the whole framing rests on -- is not controlled or varied, and no experiment compares a base model against its own post-trained descendant, which is the comparison that would establish the causal claim the method is named for.

Relevance. Two things here bear on this topic beyond the specific method. The first is a metric lesson the paper demonstrates cleanly without drawing attention to it: the same corrections are worth one to four points of AUC and five to fifteen points of true positive rate at a 5 percent false-positive threshold. AUC integrates over thresholds nobody uses, and a contamination detector is deployed at a low false-positive setting, so a paper reporting only AUC would have looked like it had a marginal result and one reporting only TPR would have looked like it had transformed the field. Both tables belong together, and the archive should ask for the operating point wherever a detector's use has an asymmetric cost. The second is what the paper establishes about the fragility of contamination detection generally, which matters because the archive already holds that a brief round of GRPO erases the signals contamination detectors rely on and that similarity-based decontamination has no principled threshold. This adds a third mechanism: ordinary post-training moves the features these detectors read, in directions consistent enough across prompt variants to be measured and corrected. Taken together, the detection side of contamination looks less like a solved measurement and more like something that must be recalibrated per model. The paper's own honesty is the caution to carry when citing it. It declines to claim the shifts it corrects are caused by post-training -- they may be dataset artifacts, decoding behaviour or the feature extractor -- and it runs no experiment comparing a base model against its own post-trained descendant, which is the comparison the framing implies. Note too that the weaker of its two detectors sits near chance on several cells before calibration, so improvements there are improvements to something that was not discriminating.

V-FiLLM: Verified Financial LLM Reasoning Benchmark

Alicia Larsen, Victoire Laurent, Aulia Kharis Rakhamsari, Lara Turgut et al. · 2026-08-11 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11047>

Generates financial reasoning benchmarks from executable computation trees over real tables so that answers are correct by construction with no model in the labelling loop, exposes four independently controllable difficulty axes, and finds that unit and scale perturbations collapse the strongest model from 98.4 percent to 3.0.

Problem. Financial reasoning over structured data demands three things at once -- retrieving values from tables, knowing domain concepts and their formulas, and composing multi-step arithmetic across monetary amounts, rates and ratios -- yet it is far less covered than STEM reasoning. Existing financial question-answering datasets are built from real documents by human annotation or model-assisted extraction, which conflates source-level noise (formatting irregularities, missing values, ambiguous phrasing) with genuine reasoning failure and makes it impossible to isolate where a model breaks. Because ground truth is written by hand or extracted heuristically, scaling such a benchmark across a range of reasoning depths is prohibitively expensive.

Contributions.

  • A benchmark-generation framework in which answers are correct by construction: computation trees over real tables are evaluated symbolically for ground truth and only then rendered into natural language, removing any model from the labelling loop and any annotation cost from scaling.
  • Four independently controllable difficulty axes -- computation depth, expression breadth, financial-concept complexity and context size -- which make it possible to attribute a failure to depth rather than to source noise.
  • A paired real-versus-simplified document design that isolates document messiness from reasoning depth, measuring real filings as roughly 20 points harder at matched depth.
  • A perturbation suite showing that failure modes are not interchangeable: distractor text costs under 20 points while unit and scale shifts collapse the strongest model from 98.4 percent to 3.0, and combined perturbations compound to 26 percent rather than averaging.
  • A verified chain-of-thought fine-tuning pipeline that keeps only traces whose final answer matches the tree-derived ground truth (88 percent retention), with the caveat that its hyperparameters were selected on the same set used to report it.

Method. Questions are generated rather than annotated. An executable computation tree is built over a real table, evaluated symbolically to give the ground-truth answer, and then rendered into a natural-language question -- so the answer is correct by construction, no model participates in labelling, and items can be produced at arbitrary scale without inheriting any generator's error rate. Four axes of difficulty are independently controllable: computation depth (the number of chained retrieval and arithmetic steps), expression breadth, financial-concept complexity, and context size. Two document types are used, real 10-Q filings which support trees to depth 4, and simplified financial statements which admit depths to 8, so the same reasoning depth can be compared under messy and clean layouts. A multi-turn variant decomposes each question into sequential sub-queries, which exposes intermediate reasoning failures that end-to-end scoring cannot see. Robustness is tested with six perturbation types applied to the spreadsheet -- missing values, garbage values, OCR look-alike character corruption, cross-sheet contamination, distractor information, and unit or scale shifts -- plus a combined condition, at 100 questions per cell. A separate fine-tuning experiment builds verified chain-of-thought traces by generating a trace per training item and keeping only those whose final answer matches the tree-derived ground truth (608 retained from 688, 88 percent), then fine-tunes a 4B model with LoRA adapters at 0.50 percent trainable parameters, sweeping rank, scaling factor and dropout.

Results. On 250 mixed-depth questions across six models, rankings are stable across both document types, with the strongest model at 98.4 and 97.6 percent. Reasoning depth is the primary limiting factor and its effect is large: on simplified statements the strongest model falls from 84.0 percent at depth 1 to 55.0 at depth 8, and the second model from 77.0 to 26.0, which also flips their ranking relative to the easy questions. On real filings both models are near-perfect at depths 1 and 2 and drop to 60-72 percent at depths 3 and 4, and at matched depth the real filings run about 20 points harder than the simplified ones -- the price of footnotes, messy layouts and mixed units. The robustness results are the paper's most striking finding and split sharply by perturbation type. Distractor information is nearly harmless, costing under 20 points on real filings, so models ignore irrelevant text well. Missing values, garbage text, OCR corruption and cross-sheet contamination each leave scores between 70 and 85 percent on real filings -- degradation without collapse. Unit and scale shifts are categorically different: they drive the strongest model from 98.4 percent to 3.0 and the second from 96.4 to 22.0, which the authors read as both models reading numbers without checking the unit labels above them. Combining all perturbations gives 26.0 and 17.0 percent, worse than almost any single condition, and the gap between the two models narrows to 9 points -- so error types compound rather than substitute, and a better table-reader's advantage does not survive enough simultaneous noise. Average degradation is 27.3 and 29.6 points on real filings and 35.7 and 47.0 on simplified ones. LoRA fine-tuning on verified traces raises held-out accuracy from 81.1 to 85.6 percent, with accuracy most sensitive to the ratio of scaling factor to rank -- any ratio other than one erases the gain entirely. Transfer to an external financial dataset is reported explicitly as a proof of concept rather than a result: 32 of 100 against a baseline of 27.

Limitations. Stated. Final answers are correct by construction but the questions are not guaranteed to be, since the underlying mathematical steps may be rendered into English ambiguously or without sufficient context. Compute constraints limit the study to six models, English-language documents and a single table-formatting convention, with no repeated trials over the same questions -- the authors name increasing the number of tests and running multiple trials as future work for statistical significance. Most consequentially, the LoRA hyperparameter sweep was conducted on the same 90-problem held-out set used to report the result, so the authors state the gains should be read as preliminary evidence rather than as a measured improvement; the external-dataset check exists specifically to test whether that improvement was overfitting, and its 5-point movement on 100 items is described as a proof of concept. Not stated but worth noticing: the depth curves rest on 100 questions per cell with no intervals, and the two models compared under perturbation are a subset of the six benchmarked, so the robustness conclusions are about that pair. The generated-question design removes annotation noise but substitutes a different constraint -- what can be asked is what a computation tree over the table can express, which is arithmetic composition over retrieved values rather than the interpretive judgement much financial analysis involves.

Relevance. Two design choices here are worth generalising beyond finance. The first is that ground truth is computed rather than written: a computation tree over a real table is evaluated symbolically and only then rendered into a question, so the label cannot inherit an annotator's error or a generator model's error rate, and difficulty becomes a dial rather than a property of whatever documents happened to be available. That is the same move the archive's industrial-safety benchmark makes with scene graphs, and the pair together suggest a general recipe -- make the answer a function of a structure you control, and the question the thing you render. The second is the paired real-versus-simplified design, which isolates document messiness from reasoning depth and quantifies it at about 20 points at matched depth. Without that pairing, a drop with depth on real documents would be uninterpretable. The robustness result is the substantive contribution for this topic, because it shows failure modes are not fungible. Distractor text costs under 20 points, four other perturbations leave scores between 70 and 85, and unit or scale shift takes the strongest model from 98.4 percent to 3.0 -- a collapse the authors attribute to models reading numbers without consulting the unit labels above them. An aggregate robustness score averaging those seven conditions would report a 27-point drop and conceal that one of them is catastrophic and the rest are not. The compounding result matters too: combining all perturbations lands below almost every individual one and narrows the gap between the two models to 9 points, so robustness rankings measured under single perturbations do not predict behaviour under several. Finally, the paper is a usable example of a failure the archive should watch for and of the right way to disclose it -- LoRA hyperparameters were swept on the same 90-item set the improvement is reported on, the authors say so, and they run an external check specifically to test whether the gain was overfitting rather than presenting the transfer number as a second result.

MathShikkha: A Controlled Study of Answer-Only and Chain-of-Thought Supervision for Bangla Mathematical Reasoning in Small Language Models

Rahma Simin Ali, Jawad Hossain · 2026-08-09 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08503>

Compares chain-of-thought against answer-only supervision under a protocol where the two conditions differ in nothing but the training target, and finds the rationales buy nothing in-domain for strong backbones while buying 20 to 28 points out of domain -- with a human study attributing the measurable effect to language adherence and inspectability rather than to better reasoning.

Problem. Rationale supervision is widely believed to improve reasoning in small models, but the overwhelming majority of the evidence compares chain-of-thought-tuned models against models with no fine-tuning at all rather than against a fine-tuned control. That comparison cannot separate the value of the rationales from the value of fine-tuning on the task, and it is exactly the comparison that matters for a practitioner deciding whether to pay a large token cost at inference. The setting is Bangla mathematics, where existing small models skip steps, mix languages, misread word-problem semantics or produce fluent but invalid reasoning, and where relying on a proprietary teacher at inference time is impractical.

Contributions.

  • A matched comparison that isolates supervision type by holding the split, loss masking, decoding and scoring pipeline identical across conditions, together with an explicit statement of which common uncontrolled variants can reverse the sign of the measured effect.
  • The in-domain finding that rationale supervision buys nothing over answer-only fine-tuning for three stronger backbones -- negative point estimates, intervals spanning zero, and a second seed in which answer-only significantly wins -- at 15 to 52 times the inference token cost.
  • The out-of-domain reversal on a three-stage contamination-audited benchmark, where rationale supervision wins by 20 to 28 points for all four models and answer-only fine-tuning actively degrades three of four below their own untuned baselines.
  • Evidence that the advantage scales with reasoning depth, with the answer-only condition collapsing from 56.6 to 16.7 percent across one to four reasoning steps while the rationale condition degrades gracefully.
  • A human study with external adjudication and inter-annotator agreement from 0.760 to 1.000 finding no significant improvement on reasoning-content criteria, and locating the measurable effect of rationale supervision in target-language adherence and inspectability instead.
  • A three-stage contamination audit including a number-and-variable-masked similarity pass that catches same-template-different-numbers leakage, reported with its one flagged case examined and dismissed by hand.

Method. A Bangla mathematical reasoning dataset is built with teacher-generated rationales, and four 4B-to-7B students are fine-tuned under a matched protocol whose whole point is that the two conditions differ only in the assistant training target. Held constant: the train/validation split, loaded once and shared rather than regenerated per condition; response-only loss masking for both; greedy decoding for both; and a single type-safe answer-normalisation and scoring pipeline applied uniformly across every model and condition. The paper states explicitly which uncontrolled variants would confound supervision type with training and scoring differences -- full-sequence loss for one condition against response-only for the other, or stochastic decoding for one against greedy for the other -- and notes these can reverse the apparent sign of the effect. Significance uses paired bootstrap confidence intervals with 10,000 resamples applying the same resampled indices to both conditions, and exact McNemar tests on the discordant pairs of the paired test set. In-domain results are replicated under a second seed. Out-of-domain evaluation uses an external school-textbook benchmark preceded by a three-stage contamination audit against all 1,030 training problems: exact normalised-text overlap, character-level near-duplicate similarity, and similarity after masking all numbers and variables to catch same-template-different-numbers leakage. Each model's conditions are scored on the common set of items all its conditions generated. A human study then compares three conditions -- base model under rationale-style prompting, answer-only fine-tuned, and rationale fine-tuned -- across eight criteria scored by two annotators with independent external-expert adjudication.

Results. In-domain, rationale supervision provides no significant improvement over answer-only fine-tuning for the three stronger backbones, and the point estimates are negative for all three (-2.06, -3.09, -5.15 points), with every bootstrap interval including zero and McNemar p at or above 0.17 -- despite generating 15 to 52 times more tokens (mean 382.7 against 23.2 for one model, 372.6 against 7.1 for another). The weakest 4B backbone is the exception and gains 18.56 points at p below 0.0001. The seed replication makes the null harder rather than easier to dismiss: under a second seed the same comparison gives -10.99 points at p = 0.0001, so answer-only supervision significantly wins. Out of domain the pattern inverts completely. On the contamination-audited external benchmark -- where the audit's first two stages flagged nothing, the third flagged one problem confirmed by inspection as a false positive, and maximum problem-level similarity to any training item was 0.84 with a median of 0.53 -- rationale supervision beats matched answer-only supervision for all four models by 20.14, 28.10, 27.08 and 23.61 points, all at p below 0.0001. More striking, answer-only fine-tuning drives external accuracy below the non-fine-tuned base model for three of four: 68.75 to 46.53 percent, 58.55 to 37.70, and 67.19 to 45.05, while rationale supervision preserves or improves it for all four. The advantage concentrates with reasoning depth: on one model, answer-only falls from 56.6 percent on one-step problems to 16.7 on four-step ones while rationale supervision holds 72.4 to 58.3; on another, 50.3 to 8.3 against 62.8 to 66.7. The human study is the result that constrains the interpretation. With inter-annotator agreement from 0.760 to 1.000 across eight criteria before adjudication, it finds no significant improvement of rationale supervision over the base model on the reasoning-content criteria -- arithmetic correctness, logical validity, problem grounding, answer consistency, completeness. Its measurable effects are target-language adherence and producing inspectable reasoning at all. A Bangla-specific 9B model evaluated under the identical pipeline reaches 48.80 percent with few-shot prompting, slightly above its own rationale fine-tuning at 45.36 and broadly comparable to the best of the distilled 4B-7B models at 48.11 -- so a model already instruction-tuned for the language gains least from further rationale distillation, mirroring the finding that the weakest general backbone gains most.

Limitations. Stated or directly visible. The in-domain null is on one 291-item test set and its sign varies by seed, which the authors report rather than selecting the favourable run. Fine-tuning shows mild overfitting -- validation loss rises while training loss falls after an intermediate stage -- and the evaluated checkpoint is selected by lowest validation loss, which is a defensible choice made on the same validation split both conditions share. Some models fall slightly below the full external item count due to non-generating items, and the authors score each model on the common set its conditions all produced rather than imputing. A small fraction of one model's outputs were degenerate or truncated at the generation ceiling and are scored incorrect. Not stated but worth noticing: the two primary human annotators are co-authors, with an external expert used for adjudication rather than for independent scoring, so the reasoning-quality result rests on annotators who knew the study's structure even if not the condition labels. The rationales come from a single proprietary teacher, so 'what rationale supervision buys' here is what that teacher's Bangla rationales buy. Four backbones and 1,030 training problems in one language and one subject support the whole conclusion, and the capability-dependence thesis -- weakest backbone gains most in-domain, most language-adapted model gains least -- is an interpretation of four points.

Relevance. The single most useful thing here is the demonstration that in-domain accuracy cannot detect what rationale supervision buys, and that the sign of the in-domain comparison is not even stable. Three stronger backbones show negative point estimates in-domain with intervals spanning zero, and under a second seed the answer-only condition significantly wins by 10.99 points -- while the same models gain 20 to 28 points out of domain. A study reporting only in-domain results, or only one seed, could have concluded either that rationales are useless or that they are harmful, and both would have been wrong about the thing practitioners care about. The out-of-domain half also produces the archive's clearest case of a fine-tuning procedure that improves the reported metric while damaging the model: answer-only supervision drives external accuracy below the untuned base for three of four models, one falling from 68.75 to 46.53 percent. In-domain evaluation of a fine-tuned model measures fit to the training distribution and can move in the opposite direction to capability. The human study is what makes the paper more than a benchmark result. With agreement from 0.760 to 1.000 across eight criteria and external adjudication, it finds no significant improvement on any reasoning-content criterion -- arithmetic correctness, logical validity, problem grounding, completeness -- and attributes the measurable effect to target-language adherence and to producing inspectable reasoning at all. So a 20-point accuracy gain coexists with no detectable improvement in the quality of the reasoning that produced it, which sharpens the archive's standing position that final-answer accuracy is an asymmetric diagnostic. The contamination audit is worth copying: three stages, the third masking all numbers and variables to catch same-template-different-numbers leakage, with the single flagged case inspected by hand and reported as a false positive, and the maximum and median similarities given rather than only a pass/fail.

MedCalc-R1: Knowledge-Guided Reward Framework for Medical Mathematical Reasoning

Haotian Wang, Lian Yan, Xingzhi Yao, Fanshu Meng et al. · 2026-08-09 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08623>

Replaces the single tolerance threshold that RLVR uses to score floating-point answers with a hybrid reward pairing a hard clinical-safety constraint against a continuous precision-sensitive term, and adds a reward for stating the computational formula explicitly so an external verifier can check it.

Problem. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards scores floating-point results against a tolerance band, and that single threshold has to do two incompatible jobs. Set it strictly and the reward becomes sparse: the policy receives almost no gradient signal, stays tethered to its supervised initialisation and stagnates. Set it leniently and near-misses and genuine successes score alike, injecting stochastic noise that produces high-variance oscillations and destabilises the update. Neither setting is calibratable in general, and in a clinical calculation setting the cost of the two errors is not symmetric -- a value outside a safety interval is a different kind of wrong from one merely imprecise.

Contributions.

  • A diagnosis of the tolerance threshold as a single parameter forced to serve two purposes -- keeping the reward dense enough to learn from, and keeping it discriminative enough to be meaningful -- with the two failure modes demonstrated by a sweep across four orders of magnitude and read off the KL trajectory as stagnation against oscillation.
  • A hybrid soft-hard reward that separates those roles: a hard constraint on a domain safety interval supplying direction, and a continuous precision-sensitive term supplying dense gradient within it, worth 11.63 points over the best static threshold.
  • A knowledge verification reward requiring the computational formula to be stated explicitly and checked by an external verifier, which raises formula recall accuracy alongside task accuracy rather than only the latter.
  • An ablation showing the two mechanisms are complementary rather than additive (4.49 and 5.06 points separately, 11.74 together) and that their contributions split cleanly by task type, with equation tasks depending on formula verification and rule-based tasks on the hybrid reward.
  • Reporting of the subtasks where the framework does not help, with an attribution -- hierarchical constraints and discrete rule enumeration that the reward mechanisms do not reach.

Method. Two reward mechanisms are combined under GRPO. The hybrid soft-hard scheme separates the threshold's two jobs: a hard constraint anchored on a clinical safety threshold ensures predictions land in a feasible region at all, and a continuous precision-sensitive soft reward then guides learning within that region, giving dense feedback where a binary tolerance gives none -- a coarse-to-fine arrangement in which the hard term supplies direction and the soft term supplies gradient. The knowledge verification reward requires the model to generate its computational formula explicitly rather than only its numeric answer, and that formula is checked by an external verifier, which is intended both to make the reasoning inspectable and to prevent formula hallucination and catastrophic forgetting of domain knowledge. Training is two-stage, supervised fine-tuning followed by GRPO at a rollout size of 5, with reward weighting coefficients all set to 1.0 deliberately to avoid task-specific tuning, and the formula verifier instantiated as a separate 14B model. Evaluation covers a clinical calculation benchmark spanning equation-based subtasks (laboratory, physical, date, dosage) and rule-based ones (risk, severity, diagnosis), chosen because it is the only available dataset containing both calculation traces and safety intervals. Controlled analyses sweep the tolerance threshold across four orders of magnitude against the hybrid scheme, and compare four policy-optimisation algorithms on the same setup.

Results. The hybrid reward beats every static tolerance setting by a wide margin on floating-point tasks: 37.90 percent against 23.25 to 25.00 across thresholds spanning four orders of magnitude, an 11.63-point improvement over the best static baseline, and the sweep shows the two failure modes the design predicts -- the strictest threshold produces the lowest KL trajectory, indicating the policy never leaves its initialisation, while the most lenient produces high-variance oscillation. The authors read their method's higher KL divergence as effective exploration rather than instability, which is an interpretation rather than a measurement but is at least consistent with the accuracy result. On the full benchmark the 3B model reaches 51.34 percent average against 40.65 for the same backbone fine-tuned on medical numerical reasoning, and the 1.5B model reaches 42.36 against 33.68. The gains concentrate where the safety framing bites: on dosage calculation the 1.5B and 3B models reach 90.00 and 87.50, well above a proprietary reasoning baseline, and on diagnosis the 1.5B model reaches 73.33. The ablation shows the two mechanisms are complementary rather than additive -- removing the knowledge reward costs 4.49 points, removing the hybrid reward costs 5.06, and removing both costs 11.74, which is more than their sum. It also shows a clean task-specific split: equation-based tasks are highly sensitive to the knowledge reward (down 6.60 without it against 0.79 for rule-based tasks), consistent with explicit formula verification suppressing formula hallucination, while rule-based tasks depend predominantly on the hybrid reward (down 4.73 against 5.25 for equation tasks). Formula recall accuracy rises from 53.91 to 57.92 alongside the accuracy gain, so the explicit formulas are more often right and not merely more often present. The paper also reports where it does not work: severity classification remains weak, which the authors attribute to hierarchical constraints and discrete rule enumeration that the reward mechanisms do not reach, and small models remain unstable on date calculations.

Limitations. Stated. Evaluation is confined to a single benchmark because it is the only dataset containing both calculation traces and the safety intervals the hard constraint requires, so generalisability is untested by construction rather than by omission. The framework is text-only while real clinical assessment synthesises imaging and vital signs. And the knowledge verification module depends on an external verifier, here a separate 14B model, which is a component whose own accuracy bounds the reward it supplies. Not stated but worth noticing: that verifier's error rate is never measured, so a formula reward computed by a model is subject to the same validity questions the archive raises about any model-supplied reward, and no human check of the verified formulas is reported. Formula recall is itself scored by a proprietary judge. The reward coefficients are fixed at 1.0 to avoid task-specific tuning, which is honest but means no sensitivity analysis over them exists, and the hard-constraint magnitudes and soft temperature are set by hand. Main-table numbers are single runs without seeds or intervals, and several of the strongest results -- dosage at 90.00, diagnosis at 73.33 -- sit on subtasks with 40 and 60 test instances.

Relevance. The threshold sweep is the part that generalises beyond clinical calculation, and it is a measurement of something the archive usually treats as a setup detail. Scoring a continuous answer against a tolerance band means one parameter simultaneously decides how often the reward fires and what counts as correct, and this paper shows the two requirements pull in opposite directions across four orders of magnitude: the strictest setting leaves the policy at its initialisation, visible as the lowest KL trajectory, while the most lenient produces high-variance oscillation, and every static setting lands between 23.25 and 25.00 percent against 37.90 for the split design. Any archived result that scores numerical answers by tolerance has that parameter buried in it, and the sweep here suggests the reported number can move by more than ten points on the threshold alone. The same logic applies to evaluation and not only to training: a tolerance chosen for reporting is a choice about what counts as a correct answer, and it is rarely justified. The second transferable observation is the task-type split in the ablation. Equation-based subtasks lose 6.60 points without the formula-verification reward while rule-based ones lose 0.79, and rule-based subtasks lose 4.73 without the hybrid reward while equation ones lose 5.25 -- so the two mechanisms are not interchangeable improvements but address different failure modes, and an aggregate ablation number would have shown two components each worth about five points and concealed that they are doing unrelated work. The caution for citing this is that the formula reward is computed by a separate model whose own accuracy is never measured, and formula recall is scored by a proprietary judge, so the interpretability claim rests on model judgement that the paper does not validate.

When Is Benchmark Contamination Detectable? Information Limits and Power-Calibrated Audits

Ibne Farabi Shihab, Sanjeda Akter, Anuj Sharma · 2026-08-08 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07914>

Casts benchmark contamination auditing as sparse-mixture detection, proves that detectability is governed by the single quantity alpha*rho*sqrt(m), and shows empirically that the resulting power predictions transport while the sample-size budgets derived from them do not.

Problem. A behavioural contamination detector that returns 'no evidence' has said one of two things — the benchmark is clean, or the audit had too little power — and nothing in the current literature distinguishes them. A 25-model study found only 201 of 335 audit outcomes correct, establishing that audits fail without saying when they will. What is missing is audit power: how many independent items must be probed to detect a small exposed fraction, and which fractions a non-rejection actually rules out.

Contributions.

  • A finite-sample second-moment bound showing every level-tau audit's power is capped by alpha*rho*sqrt(m), with rho^2 = chi^2(P_1 || P_0) the behavioural separability
  • Proposition 5: any scalar detector's efficacy e_f is at most rho and is estimable from matched controls before the audit is run
  • A sample-split certificate that lower-bounds the exposed fraction alpha distribution-free, with no orientation assumption
  • An empirical result reported as a failure as well as a success: calibration efficacy predicts power curves but the Gaussian budget it prescribes is miscalibrated, and a simulation-based planner repairs it

Method. A benchmark item carries a hidden exposure indicator Z, and under a matched partial-contamination channel the probe outcome is P_0 given Z=0 and P_1 given Z=1, so uniformly sampling items gives the sparse mixture Q_alpha = (1-alpha)P_0 + alpha*P_1. All repeated decodes, perturbations and token-level features of one item count as a single joint outcome, so m is a count of independent items rather than of probes. Theorem 3 bounds type-I plus type-II error below by 1 - min{1, C_m(alpha, rho)} with C_m = 0.5*{(1+alpha^2 rho^2)^m - 1}^{1/2}, and Corollary 4 turns it into a detection boundary: if alpha_m*rho_m*sqrt(m) tends to zero no asymptotic level-tau audit has power beyond its false-positive rate, so nontrivial power requires alpha = Omega(1/(rho*sqrt(m))). The bound is attained by the centred likelihood-ratio score, making it a minimax rather than a loose limit. Proposition 5 converts a practical scalar score f into a power diagnostic through its efficacy e_f = |E_1 f - E_0 f| / sd_0(f), proves e_f <= rho with equality only when f is affine in the density ratio, and gives the planning equation m_target = ((z_{1-tau} + z_{1-beta}) / (alpha * e_f))^2 -- estimable from matched controls rather than from an unattainable channel oracle. The audit itself splits data by original document into a score-training split, a calibration split and an untouched audit split; the confirmatory decision is a one-sided two-sample permutation test with the score, its orientation and the budget all frozen before the audit split is opened. Four validity gates are part of the contract: blind separation (a text-only classifier must not separate the pools), clean-channel transport, channel stability across alpha, and an independence unit counting source-item clusters. Evaluation A uses checkpoint-matched channels; Evaluation B injects SQuAD items into a Pile background and continues pretraining pythia-160m, crossing the injection with four mechanisms at four copies per item -- exact question-answer text, a validated paraphrase, a word-order shuffle, and answer-only -- against a matched clean continuation from the same seed. Five gray-box probe families are scored: mean loss, zlib-normalized loss, Min-K% Prob, Min-K%++ and neighbourhood loss.

Results. Two-sided, and the paper reports the failures as primary endpoints. Frozen calibration efficacy predicts held-out permutation power well across six model-by-corpus channels: R^2 0.83-0.98, MAE 0.03-0.11, slope 0.91-1.04, with empirical size at alpha=0 of 0.009-0.045, so the test is valid and somewhat conservative. The budgets derived from those curves do not transport. The pre-specified pooled calibration-to-audit scaling claim is withdrawn, with efficacy-transport ratios at a median of 2.23x against a 2x tolerance fixed before the numbers were seen; with an 800-item calibration the ratios shrink to 1.03-1.50, and on the gate-passing channels raw efficacy ratios are 1.02-1.35. So efficacy itself largely transports and the Gaussian inversion is what breaks: the plug-in budget fails in 9 of 9 gate-passing channels, with m_pred/m_star of 0.54-0.86 and observed power 0.01-0.74 where 0.80 was prescribed. A two-stage planner that resamples the score laws and runs the deployed permutation test at each candidate m is uniformly conservative (m_plan/m_star 1.00-2.00, median 1.36), delivers 0.785-0.92 power with 0.80 or better in 6 of 8 channels and zero clear failures, and abstains on the one channel whose probe does not transport. The prevalence certificate is valid and vacuous at audit scale: it never fires at m=512 in any of the six channels, and certifying alpha of 0.1-0.2 would need balanced groups of roughly 3,500-79,000 items. The five-seed paired injection study recovers a mechanism ordering from the causal contrast D_s -- exact 1.01 +/- 0.07, paraphrase 0.70 +/- 0.09, surface shuffle 0.44 +/- 0.04 -- while answer-only comes in at -0.01 +/- 0.07 with every per-seed interval covering zero, so its apparent raw efficacy of 0.19 is baseline drift that the paired design exists to catch. The validity gates do real work: every checkpoint-free Evaluation A channel fails blind separation at text-only AUC 0.95, meaning content-confounded separability rather than membership, while the same-corpus continued-pretraining channels repair it at blind AUC 0.48-0.54 with 4 of 6 passing every gate.

Limitations. The paper's own limitations section is unusually complete and should be read as part of the result. The mixture channel is an auditable modelling assumption rather than a law of training: contaminated examples can affect unexposed ones and post-training can alter both the clean and seen channels, and the stability diagnostics can reveal large violations but cannot prove transportability. The lower bound is pointwise in a chosen behavioural access channel and says nothing about direct corpus search, cryptographic provenance or watermarking. The matching optimality is local and assumes finite chi-square divergence. The certificate needs independent matched controls and a score learned on separate data, which an external auditor of a closed model generally cannot construct, so neither efficacy nor the exposed fraction is identified without extra assumptions. Six named gaps are open and unexecuted, including the locked external validation on the 25-model release that motivates the paper. A reader should add that the causal exposure claim rests on one base checkpoint (pythia-160m), one benchmark (SQuAD), one copy count and one protocol; that 'Pile-trained' does not certify the base never saw SQuAD, so only the incremental effect of the paired continuations is claimed; that the mechanism arms are not dose-matched, so the ordering is descriptive rather than a causal comparison; and that the three adjacent mechanism differences are significant per seed by sign-test but not after Holm correction.

Relevance. The archive already holds the result that a brief round of GRPO erases the signals contamination detectors rely on, and treats that as a reason to distrust any benchmark number. This supplies the missing half: an information-theoretic statement of when a detector could have worked at all, in one estimable quantity, plus the finding that a null audit is uninterpretable unless its efficacy, budget and validity gates are reported alongside it. It also gives the archive its first measured mechanism ordering for contamination -- verbatim above paraphrase above surface perturbation, with answer-only exposure indistinguishable from drift -- which bears directly on whether the reasoning benchmarks here are exposed in a way any behavioural probe could see.

Zero Gap Is Not Restoration: Stratified Per-Question Probability Evaluation and Step-wise Mitigation of Benchmark Contamination

Ruijie Hou, Yueyang Jiao, Zhao Wang, Yingming Li · 2026-08-07 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.07341>

Shows that the standard metric for judging contamination-mitigation strategies can be scored by cancellation rather than by restoration, replaces it with a per-question stratified metric under which the published ranking reverses, and proposes a mitigation that decides its intervention during decoding instead of from a prior estimate.

Problem. Test data leaks into pretraining corpora and inflates benchmark scores. One response is contamination-mitigation evaluation: build no new dataset, but intervene during decoding to suppress memorization and restore the contaminated model's genuine capability. Whether a mitigation strategy actually restores anything must be checked against a reliable metric, and the metric used throughout that literature -- the gap of aggregate performance -- assigns each question a discrete correct/incorrect mark, averages over the dataset, and takes the difference from the clean model's average. Both halves of that are broken, and because the metric shapes how strategies are designed, the damage is not confined to the scoreboard.

Contributions.

  • A demonstration that a zero aggregate gap certifies nothing: over-suppression on some questions cancels under-suppression on others
  • SA-PPG, a metric that reads zero if and only if every question's solve probability matches the clean model's, and that is stratified so a trivial strategy cannot score well by chasing the clean model's most frequent value
  • A rank reversal on published strategies: the method that looks near-perfect under the old metric is barely better than no intervention under the new one
  • RailCap, a mitigation that replaces the pre-hoc contamination estimate with step-wise supervision during generation

Method. The metric is rebuilt in three steps. First the readout: a single sampled response marked 0/1 is a Bernoulli draw, and what stabilizes as samples grow is the solve probability, so each question's performance is estimated from 50 samples. Second the aggregation order: taking absolute values per question and then averaging gives A-PPG, which is zero exactly when every question is restored, whereas averaging first and differencing gives the old metric and equals the absolute difference of the two error components. Written out, A-PPG is the sum of under-suppression (residual contamination) and over-suppression (collateral damage) while the old metric is their difference -- so the old metric reaches zero whenever the two are equal, which cancellation alone achieves. Third, equal weighting: when the clean model is weak, most questions sit near zero solve probability, and a strategy that simply drives the contaminated model to fail everything scores a zero gap on that majority while large gaps on the capable minority are diluted. SA-PPG therefore bins questions by the clean model's own solve probability into 50 equal-width groups, averages within each group and then across groups, so a strategy must restore at every capability level. RailCap, the proposed mitigation, is built on two observations about the contaminated model's own generation: on leaked questions its sampled responses collapse onto its greedy trajectory while on unleaked ones they disperse, and at the decoding steps where clean and contaminated models diverge, the clean model's choice is the contaminated model's runner-up about half the time. So it greedy-decodes once per question, indexes every n-gram window of that trajectory to its successor token, and during sampling caps that successor's logit to the current second-largest whenever the last n tokens fall back onto the trajectory. Suppression accumulates step by step and how much any question receives is decided online rather than allocated in advance by a one-shot estimate. Contamination is simulated on Llama-2-7B, Gemma-4-E2B and Pythia-12B: fine-tune the base on training data to get the clean model, then continue from it with test-split questions mixed in to get the contaminated one, over GSM8K and a paraphrased version whose wording is rewritten but whose numbers and answers are unchanged.

Results. The rank reversal is the headline and it is complete. On Llama-2 with GSM8K, LNE-blocking reads 0.0235 under the old metric against 0.3192 for no intervention at all, and is the best strategy in that column; under SA-PPG it reads 0.2932 against 0.3261 for no intervention, which is barely a difference, and it falls behind two other strategies. RailCap, which is not best under the old metric at 0.0728, is best under SA-PPG at 0.1914. Same responses, same models, same strategies -- only the metric changes, and the verdict inverts. The decomposition shows where the old metric hides the error: LNE-blocking's over-suppression component is 2.0 times RailCap's, yet its aggregate reading is 3.8 times better, because the extra collateral damage cancels against the residual contamination and pushes the number toward zero. The readout correction is worth separating from the aggregation correction, and the paper measures both. Drawing two independent sampling batches from the same clean model, the mean per-question gap between batches is 0.190 under the single-sample discrete readout and 0.041 under the 50-sample solve probability -- so a 0/1 readout does not even reproduce between two evaluations of one model. And a synthetic All-Zero strategy that makes the contaminated model fail every question scores 0.2190 under equal-weight A-PPG, better than no intervention (0.3793) and better than two published strategies, while stratification ranks it worst of all at 0.4903. RailCap attains the lowest SA-PPG in all six model-by-domain settings. The baselines are consistent and modest: TED is nearly indistinguishable from no intervention everywhere, and LNE-blocking is worse than no intervention on all three models in the paraphrase domain, which the paper reads as a one-shot pre-hoc estimate degrading when the questions seen at inference differ from the contaminated ones.

Limitations. No limitations section. The load-bearing one a reader must supply: SA-PPG is defined relative to a clean model, and outside a simulation that reference does not exist -- the whole evaluation depends on having trained a matched uncontaminated twin, so this is a metric for comparing mitigation strategies in the lab and not for auditing a deployed model. Contamination is likewise simulated by deliberately mixing test items into a fine-tuning corpus, which produces verbatim-style exposure; the archive's companion entry on audit power measures that verbatim, paraphrase and surface-shuffle exposure produce sharply different behavioural signals, so results established on the strongest mechanism should not be read as covering the others -- the paraphrase domain here mitigates that but keeps numbers and answers identical, so it varies wording only. Everything is GSM8K or a rewrite of it, so the capability distribution that stratification is defined over is one benchmark's. RailCap needs a greedy decode per question and logit access at every step, ruling out closed models, and its n-gram threshold is a single setting whose sensitivity is unreported. Finally, the clean models are themselves LoRA fine-tunes at one hyperparameter setting, and the claim that base checkpoints are uncontaminated is verifiable only for Pythia.

Relevance. This archive already holds the result that a brief round of GRPO erases the signals contamination detectors rely on, and treats it as a reason to distrust benchmark numbers. This attacks the layer above: even where mitigation is attempted, the metric everyone used to judge it can be satisfied by damaging the model as much as the contamination inflated it. The All-Zero control is the cleanest statement of that -- a strategy that makes the model fail everything outscores two published methods. It also gives the archive a measurement it lacked, that a single-sample 0/1 readout does not reproduce between two evaluations of the same model (mean per-question gap 0.190 against 0.041 for a 50-sample probability), which bears on far more of this literature than contamination work.

Problem. LLM judges tend to produce particular scores regardless of the evaluated text, a scoring bias that corrupts their use as evaluation metrics. Existing calibration methods do not isolate where the bias comes from.

Contributions.

  • A random-number-generation probe that isolates an LLM's latent numerical bias independently of evaluated content

  • Evidence that scoring bias varies across models, tasks and score ranges

Because a random-number request has no correct answer, any deviation is attributable to the model rather than to the content — that is what makes the measurement a clean bias probe. A definition of the downstream task the judge will be used for is added to the random-number prompt, yielding a task-specific latent bias. At evaluation time the token generation probabilities for a given input are rectified using the measured bias.

Results. On four tasks — LLM alignment evaluation, summarization evaluation, Semantic Textual Similarity and Semantic Textual Relatedness — the method outperforms an undebiased LLM and previous calibration methods. Scoring bias is confirmed to vary across LLMs, tasks and score ranges.

Limitations. No numeric margins in the abstract, and the models are not named. The method assumes a uniform distribution is the correct reference for a random-number request, which is a modelling choice rather than a fact about the intended judge behaviour. Rectification needs access to token generation probabilities, so it does not apply to judges behind APIs that do not expose logprobs. Bias varying by score range means a single measured distribution may not correct the tails.

Relevance. LLM judges are how much reasoning work gets scored once the answer is not a checkable string, so a bias in the judge is a bias in the field's measurements. The useful part for this archive is the probe rather than the fix: a random-number request has no correct answer, so deviation from uniform is a content-free estimate of the judge's prior, and it is cheap enough to run as a routine control. The finding that bias varies by score range matters most for reasoning evaluation, where scores cluster high and the compressed top of the scale is exactly where discrimination is needed.

DASH: Divergence-Adaptive Supervision Horizons for On-Policy Self-Distillation of Reasoning Models

ZhiYan Hou, Xinyu Tang, Hongyan An, Jianjin Zhang et al. · 2026-08-06 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06243>

Weights on-policy self-distillation supervision by how each local teacher-student divergence compares to the sequence mean, gating backward multi-step aggregation on that comparison.

Problem. RLVR signals are sparse and sequence-level. On-policy self-distillation densifies them by querying a privileged teacher at student-visited prefixes, but standard OPSD gives every local divergence the same coefficient regardless of position or of the divergence sequence it sits in. The same divergence magnitude can follow different discrepancy histories, and a local scalar alone cannot distinguish those temporal contexts.

Contributions.

  • The observation that standard OPSD ignores the temporal structure of the divergence sequence, weighting equal magnitudes equally regardless of history
  • DASH: an adaptive propagation gate from the local-versus-sequence-mean divergence gap
  • Gate-controlled backward multi-step aggregation of token-level supervision weights
  • Improvement over matched vanilla OPSD on every benchmark at all three scales, with no additional teacher or student forward pass

Method. DASH maps the gap between each local distillation signal and the sequence-level mean to an adaptive propagation gate, then uses those gates to control backward multi-step aggregation. Token-level supervision weights therefore depend on how divergences evolve over the rollout rather than on their instantaneous size. It reuses the teacher and student distributions OPSD already computes, so it costs no extra forward pass.

Results. On three mathematical reasoning benchmarks across three model scales, DASH improves over matched vanilla OPSD reruns on every benchmark at all three scales. Effect sizes are not given in the abstract.

Limitations. No effect sizes, benchmark names or model scales in the abstract, so the improvement is directional only. The baseline is the authors' own matched OPSD reruns, which is the right comparison but leaves the absolute standing against other dense-supervision methods unstated. Requires a privileged teacher. The sequence-level mean as the reference point makes each token's weight depend on the whole rollout, so weights are not available online during generation.

Relevance. Matched on benchmark vocabulary; it contributes no evaluation method. The one point for this topic is its baseline discipline — improvements are measured against the authors' own matched OPSD reruns rather than against published numbers, which is the practice the archive's evaluation-noise thread found almost universally absent and which is what makes a same-scale comparison trustworthy.

RP-OPSD: Reasoning-Pivot-Guided On-Policy Self-Distillation for Multilingual Reasoning Transfer

Xinye Wang, Junxiao Liu, Shujian Huang · 2026-08-06 · arxiv

<https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06347>

Concentrates privileged self-distillation on reasoning pivots identified by the teacher's distributional shift when an English reference solution is added or removed, for multilingual reasoning transfer.

Problem. Reasoning transfer beyond high-resource languages is needed to extend LLM reasoning. On-policy self-distillation gives dense token-level supervision on student rollouts, but its objectives do not explicitly prioritize the reasoning signals most critical to cross-lingual transfer.

Contributions.

  • A characterization of target-language reasoning as surface text plus reasoning pivots
  • RP-OPSD: using the teacher's distributional shift with and without an English reference solution as an operational pivot proxy
  • Concentration of privileged distillation and reference anchoring on those pivots
  • Results over 17 languages and multiple difficulty levels against multilingual and OPSD baselines
  • An analysis showing the learned weighting favours reasoning-control and state-update tokens over surface realization

Method. Target-language reasoning is characterized as generating both surface text and reasoning pivots — decisions that advance or redirect the reasoning process and shape subsequent inference. RP-OPSD uses the distributional shift between matched teacher views, one with and one without an English reference solution, as an operational proxy for locating those pivots, and concentrates privileged distillation and reference anchoring there. The paired-view construction is what makes the proxy computable without pivot labels: tokens whose distribution moves when the reference is supplied are the ones the reference decides.

Results. On mathematical reasoning benchmarks covering 17 languages and multiple difficulty levels, RP-OPSD outperforms strong multilingual reasoning baselines and OPSD variants. Analysis shows it concentrates privileged distillation on reasoning-control and problem-conditioned state-update tokens while downweighting tokens that mainly support surface realization.

Limitations. No effect sizes, benchmark names or model scales in the abstract. The pivot proxy is defined by sensitivity to an English reference, so it presupposes English as the anchor and identifies pivots relative to that anchor rather than intrinsically. Restricted to mathematical reasoning. The claim that the method separates reasoning-control tokens from surface-realization tokens is an analysis of its own weights, not an independent validation.

Relevance. Matched on benchmark vocabulary rather than evaluation content. Its value here is coverage: 17 languages and stratified difficulty is a wider evaluation surface than the English math benchmarks the archive is dominated by, and it separates reasoning content from surface realization, which is a distinction English-only benchmarks cannot make because the two are confounded.

SMART: Evaluating LLMs&apos; Mathematical Reasoning via a Human Cognitive Process-Inspired Benchmark

Yujie Hou, Mei Wang, Yaoyao Zhong, Ting Zhang 0002 et al. · ACL · 2026-01-01 · dblp

<https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.1638>

Decomposes mathematical problem-solving into four cognitive dimensions after Polya and tests each separately, finding wide capability gaps that final-answer accuracy hides.

Problem. Strong benchmark performance leaves open whether it reflects genuine reasoning or superficial pattern recognition. Existing evaluation focuses either on the final answer or on intermediate steps, reducing mathematical reasoning to a shallow input-output mapping and overlooking that it is inherently multi-stage and multi-dimensional.

Contributions.

  • SMART, a benchmark decomposing mathematical problem-solving into Semantic Understanding, Mathematical Reasoning, Arithmetic Computation and Reflection/Refinement
  • Dimension-specific tasks that measure each cognitive process separately rather than through final answers
  • Evaluation of 22 open- and closed-source LLMs revealing substantial cross-dimensional capability discrepancies
  • The All-Pass Score, a metric requiring success across dimensions rather than on average

Method. SMART, inspired by Polya's problem-solving theory, decomposes mathematical problem-solving into four cognitive dimensions — Semantic Understanding, Mathematical Reasoning, Arithmetic Computation, and Reflection/Refinement — and introduces dimension-specific tasks that measure each corresponding process separately. Measuring dimensions independently is what exposes compensation, where strength in one dimension masks weakness in another under a single aggregate score. A new All-Pass Score metric requires success across dimensions rather than on average.

Results. Applied to 22 state-of-the-art open- and closed-source LLMs, SMART uncovers substantial discrepancies in capability across the four dimensions and reveals genuine weaknesses in current models, motivating the All-Pass Score.

Limitations. No numbers in the abstract, so the size of the cross-dimensional discrepancies is unstated and the models are not listed. The four-dimensional decomposition follows a theory of human problem-solving, and whether those dimensions carve model computation at its joints is assumed rather than shown. Dimension-specific tasks may themselves be confounded — a semantic-understanding task still requires reading and producing an answer.

Relevance. A direct construct-validity contribution: it argues a single math accuracy number aggregates over dimensions that can compensate for one another, and the All-Pass Score is a concrete alternative that refuses to average them. That is the same structural criticism the archive already holds from VAR-MATH, which varies problem instances, but arrives from a different direction — decomposing the task rather than perturbing it — and the two are complementary. It joins three other papers in this drain arguing that reasoning benchmarks measure something other than reasoning, alongside long.826 on perception and findings-acl.460 on code.

VisAidMath: Benchmarking Visual-Aided Mathematical Reasoning

Jingkun Ma, Runzhe Zhan, Yang Li, Di Sun et al. · ACL · 2026-01-01 · dblp

<https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2026.acl-long.1719>

Benchmarks whether multimodal models can construct visual aids for geometry problems, and finds high answer accuracy conceals near-total failure at producing or reasoning from those aids.

Problem. Advancing from passive visual perception to actively modifying visual information in service of reasoning is a hallmark capability that remains underdeveloped in large multimodal models. The deficiency is masked by metrics that prioritize final-answer accuracy, creating an illusion of competence where genuine reasoning is absent.

Contributions.

  • VisAidMath, a benchmark requiring construction of visual aids for geometric problem-solving
  • The Three-Layered Funnel Evaluation Framework separating accuracy (ACCU), valid visual aid generation (PVA) and soundness of subsequent reasoning (SPRS)
  • Identification of a 'Reasoning Illusion' where high accuracy coexists with failure to produce or use valid visual aids
  • A public evaluation platform on CodaBench

Method. Geometric problem-solving is used as a precise instrument, through tasks that require constructing visual aids. VisAidMath is the benchmark; the Three-Layered Funnel Evaluation Framework moves beyond accuracy (ACCU) to separately score the generation of valid visual aids (PVA) and the soundness of the subsequent reasoning steps (SPRS). Scoring the aid and the reasoning-from-aid separately is what makes the dissociation visible, since a model can reach the right answer without either.

Results. Experiments on state-of-the-art models including Doubao-Seed-1.6 and o4 reveal a 'Reasoning Illusion': high surface-level accuracy conceals catastrophic failure in producing valid visual aids or reasoning from them. The authors describe this as a fundamental schism between visual perception and logical deduction in modern LMMs. A public evaluation platform is provided on CodaBench.

Limitations. No numbers in the abstract, so 'catastrophic' is uncalibrated. Geometry with constructed visual aids is a narrow instrument, chosen for precision, and the schism claim is broader than that evidence. PVA and SPRS require judging validity and soundness, and how that judgement is made — human or model — is unstated, which matters because a model judge would inherit the deficiency being measured.

Relevance. Another instance of the drain's dominant finding, and one of its sharpest: the answer can be right while every intermediate artefact the answer supposedly depends on is wrong. Its layered funnel is a reusable design — score the artefact, then score reasoning from the artefact, then score the answer — and it applies wherever reasoning is supposed to route through an intermediate object. Read with long.826, which finds ARC-style failures are 80% perception rather than reasoning, the two make opposite-facing claims about the same perception/reasoning boundary: one says perception failure is misread as reasoning failure, the other says reasoning failure hides behind accuracy. Both agree the boundary is where the measurement breaks.

credit assignment31Deciding which part of a trajectory earned the reward, and across 31 sources the problem the archive's training literature keeps rediscovering under different names. The complaint is uniformly one of granularity mismatch: a policy is rewarded on whole outputs or whole trajectories while the decisions that produced the result happen at a finer scale, so nothing identifies which retrieval found the key document, which translation choice made the output unfaithful, or which of several changes packed into one code action earned the test pass. The difficulty is worst exactly where the trajectory is longest, since a sparse terminal reward over many turns supplies no supervision at the point of greatest ambiguity. The archive's methods split by where they cut. Some cut by turn, some by trajectory segment type, some by sub-parts of a single action matched across rollouts, and some by token selection -- and their reported contributions are consistent in size, typically 2 to 8 points, against ablations of the terminal signal that are typically 20 to 65. That asymmetry is the corpus's most reliable finding here: finer credit is a refinement of outcome supervision, never a substitute, and every source that removes the terminal reward finds it the largest single loss. Two design constraints follow and are stated independently by several sources. A local term should not be able to flip the sign of the trajectory-level advantage or create an update where the outcome reward is silent -- hence multiplicative rather than additive turn weighting, and gates that stop partial reference agreement overriding a wrong answer. And a criterion for allocating credit is not credited until it has been raced against random allocation at matched cost, because a randomised control routinely captures much of the benefit attributed to the targeted criterion.
construct validity28Whether a measurement is measuring the thing it is named after, and across 28 sources the archive's most frequently applied critical lens -- usually applied by the authors to their own work, which is why it appears in limitations more often than in results. The recurring failure is a correlated alternative that the design cannot exclude. A temporal-horizon steering direction also separates abstraction, urgency, strategic scope and framing, so the intervention may be steering a broad answer register rather than the named variable, and a transfer result shows the construct is involved without identifying the direction's semantic basis. A probe's margin can be generic: training the identical probe on a small model that never produced the responses reaches 0.72 because the items are by construction more ambiguous as prompts, so only the 14-point margin above that is unexplained. And a total-effect measurement can be answering a different question entirely -- a revision that turns an unparseable output into a parseable one changes the score without changing any reasoning. Two conclusions the corpus reaches are worth carrying. Validity is a property of the whole protocol rather than of the model: measured performance depends on template, decoder, controller, reducer, verifier, stopping rule and numerical settings, so a number without its protocol is not comparable to another. overthinking28Producing more reasoning than the problem needs, and across 28 sources the archive's most-attacked inefficiency. The waste is measured rather than asserted: a median of 2.7 thousand emitted tokens against a median 830 needed to reach the correct answer on one benchmark, and 11 to 29 percent token reduction at matched accuracy across three model sizes -- with the savings heavily skewed, reaching 53 percent for the top decile of problems and 81 for the top percentile. Redundancy is diffuse rather than located in an identifiable class of step, so importance criteria agree on what to keep and disagree on what to cut. The same signal that marks productive reasoning marks overthinking on retrieval-style tasks, where amplifying deliberation makes a model derive facts it should retrieve -- one source's candid failure analysis. test-time scaling28Spending more computation at inference to get a better answer, and across 28 sources a phrase covering algorithms with different statistical structure, compute accounting and failure modes -- extending one trajectory, sampling completed candidates and reducing them by voting or verification, and searching over unfinished partial states. It is not monotone in the sample count, because more candidates strengthen whatever the aggregation rule already does; the selection signal is the binding constraint past a modest budget, and where confidence does not track correctness no verifier-free rule recovers the available headroom (measured at 14 to 17 oracle points on one hard benchmark against 0.8 percent captured). Uniform spending is wasteful in both directions, and adaptive allocation is worth having but hard to learn and hard to audit. The budget is almost always spent on the output side -- re-running the model on the same input -- and input-side diversity is a direct competitor for the same calls that has rarely been benchmarked against it at matched compute. chain of thought faithfulness26Whether a stated chain of thought is an account of the process that produced the answer, and across 26 sources the archive's central negative result. The measurements are consistent: faithfulness varies substantially across tasks, with models sometimes relying heavily on the trace and sometimes largely ignoring it, and it decreases with model size and capability on most tasks studied -- so the earliest source's own conclusion is a conditional (traces can be faithful if size and task are chosen carefully) rather than an endorsement. Two structural problems recur. The standard test detects unfaithfulness only where an injected cue changes the answer, so it is silent whenever the answer is unchanged, and it intervenes on the prompt rather than on the trace. And the property is not simply desirable: a perfectly faithful agent executes a harmful action whenever its reasoning arrives at one, which makes the usual requirement self-contradictory -- faithful enough to be monitored, robust enough to override its own reasoning. One source measures that tension directly, finding the most faithful model follows tampered safe reasoning in 97.5 percent of cases while rejecting unsafe reasoning in only 12.3, higher faithfulness going with lower safety across seven models, and steering along the two directions trading them against each other by 5 to 9 points against magnitude-matched random vectors moving either by at most 1.1. The archive's operative position is that faithfulness is a relation between the trace and the answer rather than a property of the trace: monitoring works as a consistency check between the two, and a trace can be clean, correct and on-topic while the action taken is arbitrary.
advantage estimation23Deciding how much better than a baseline a rollout was, which is what a policy update is actually made of. Across 23 sources the corpus's contribution is diagnostic rather than definitional: it shows what goes wrong when the estimate is asymmetric, degenerate or unidentified. Asymmetry is the sharpest case. A regression critic that overestimates success probability gives failed rollouts large negative advantages and successes small positive ones -- measured at -0.597 against +0.227, a ratio of 2.63, growing more skewed over training -- so the update spends most of its magnitude on failures; a distributional critic brings the ratio to 1.02, and the paper notes correctly that batch-wise normalisation is an affine rescaling that cannot remove a relative asymmetry. Degeneracy is the group-relative case: a prompt whose rollouts all share a reward has zero advantage for every member and contributes no gradient, and one source measures a first stage cutting all-wrong groups by 4.95 points precisely to address it. Collapse is a third failure: in latent reasoning, within-thought answer variance exceeds between-thought variance in every setting and training makes it worse (noise-to-signal 1.54 rising to 13.10), so the thought-level advantage is estimating differences that have largely disappeared. Two further findings recur. Weighting is a bias-variance trade rather than a free improvement -- one truncation-order sweep peaks at 4 and degrades beyond it because higher moments cannot be estimated from eight rollouts, and one interpolation sweep runs 45.4, 52.1, 44.8 as its coefficient goes 0.00, 0.25, 1.00. chain of thought23The intermediate tokens a model emits before its answer, and across 23 sources an object the archive is careful to describe rather than to interpret -- one source proposes 'derivational trace' as a neutral stand-in precisely because reading these tokens as reasoning is unsupported by the available evidence and, it argues, actively harmful. What is established is largely formal and negative. Formally, generating intermediate tokens lifts the expressive ceiling of a bounded-depth transformer, with the number of steps tied to a circuit class; one archived construction realises graph traversal in two layers and a linear-step branching measure without layer normalisation, and another shows a sharpness-based bound growing linearly rather than exponentially in the relevant parameter for a parity family. Empirically the trace and the answer are separate information channels: content present in one can be absent from the other in both directions, traces can be swapped between problems or deleted with the answer unchanged, and post-training that improves accuracy need not improve the trace's semantic validity. The corpus's practical positions are that the trace is useful as a consistency check against the answer rather than as an account of computation, that its length is a compute knob with a bend in the curve, and that a large fraction of it is redundant -- with the caveat from a head-to-head comparison that redundancy is diffuse rather than located in any identifiable class of step.
pass@k21The probability that at least one of k sampled responses is correct, used across 21 sources as the archive's measure of what a model can reach as opposed to what it reliably produces. Reading it correctly is most of what the corpus has to say. Small k measures sampling efficiency and large k probes the boundary, and the two can move in opposite directions within one training run -- pass@1 rising across steps while coverage at pass@256 falls, and in one distillation study the curve crossing between k=1 and k=1024 in every setting measured. A rising curve is also compatible with within-question stability collapsing, so reporting only coverage hides that the same model now answers inconsistently. Two corrections to naive use are established. Scoring the chain as well as the answer reverses the verdict about reinforcement learning at every k, because base models win plain pass@k partly by producing wrong chains that land on right answers. And for candidate banks produced by adaptive search or dependent procedures the usual estimator is a descriptive summary of what was returned rather than an unbiased probability -- one source states this and notes most papers reporting the metric do not. The archive's practical demand is that a method report pass@k across a range rather than at one budget, since the property worth having is competitiveness with the base model uniformly across k rather than a win at either end.

The complaint is uniform: a ten-retrieval search trajectory receives one scalar and nothing distinguishes the retrieval that found the key document from one that repeated a query; the reward supervises only the answer, so a model can reach it without attending to the audio, the image or the intermediate quantities at all; and over a long tool-use trajectory it supplies no supervision at the exact point where credit is most ambiguous. Yet every source that ablates it finds it indispensable rather than merely helpful, and the archive holds no instance of dense supervision anchoring a policy on its own -- removing the terminal reward is repeatedly the largest single loss, and one system with it removed keeps perfect step-level tool selection while never terminating. The design consequence the sources converge on is that dense signals should be applied as modifiers that cannot flip a sign or create an update where the outcome reward is silent: one applies its turn-level term multiplicatively rather than additively for exactly this reason, another gates partial reference agreement so it cannot override a wrong final answer, and a third bounds a privileged correction by the magnitude of the outcome advantage. A separate limitation is that its scope is the answer: post-training against it improves solution accuracy without necessarily improving the semantic validity of the traces, which one source notes should be unsurprising given what it scores.
residual stream17The additively updated activation vector each transformer layer reads from and writes to, and in this archive the substrate every interpretability method operates on rather than an object studied in itself. What the 17 sources establish about it is mostly negative and methodological. It has no privileged basis: individual neurons are polysemantic and there is no reason to expect features here to align with the neuron basis at all, which is the premise the sparse-autoencoder literature is built on -- and that literature's own numbers show the cost, with automated interpretability scores declining from about 0.30 at layer 0 to 0.15 by layer 5 and reconstruction raising perplexity from 25 to 40 when substituted back. The causal information is often compact: replacing the whole stream at a site has a comparable effect to a low-rank patch, so the relevant subspace is small. self-correction17A model detecting and repairing its own error, and across 17 sources a capability the archive can measure the behaviour of more easily than the benefit. The central finding is a dissociation: how often a model announces a correction is anti-correlated with whether the corrections help. Against two prior supervised methods on a shared backbone, a step-level preference method scores higher on both mathematics benchmarks while self-correcting at 28.8 and 23.9 percent against 45.3 and 46.5 and recalling fewer errors, and two of its own five backbones lower their correction rate after training while improving. The diagnosis offered is behaviour collapse under supervised training, which biases a model toward following correction templates rather than selectively revising real errors. Two measurement problems follow. The behavioural metrics are defined by matching a list of signal phrases, so a model that corrects without using one is invisible to the measurement, and the training data's preferred continuations are defined by the same convention. And the construct has almost no clean measurement window: models weak enough to have headroom lack the signal, models with the signal lack the headroom, so the design space where self-correction could be measured cleanly is nearly empty -- one source's calibration-floor argument finds only one marginally viable cell with negligible held-out gain. Where the archive finds it working is as a trained selectivity rather than a prompted behaviour, with the preference formed between a repair and the continuation that would have followed had the error stood, so the signal exists only where an error does.
verification17Deciding whether a candidate solution is correct, and the hinge on which most of this archive turns: RLVR needs it to compute a reward, test-time selection needs it to choose, and process supervision needs it per step. Sixteen sources supply it from four different places, ordered here by how much they can be trusted. An oracle — a compiler, unit tests, executable symbolic templates — is exact but exists only in some domains. A trained reward or process model is general and repeatedly found miscalibrated, which is why several archived methods are explicitly verifier-free. A model asked to judge is more general still and carries its own biases, though evaluator accuracy is shown to rise monotonically with the reasoning tokens it is given. And the model's own internal state can be read: a training-free comparison of a trace's start-to-end activation delta against two class centroids, or attention-routing alignment, both predict correctness without any external checker. One theoretical result reframes what is needed: pairwise comparison better than chance, not absolute correctness judgement, is enough to drive failure probability to zero.
calibration16Whether a stated or implied probability matches the frequency it is right, used across 16 sources in three different senses that are worth keeping apart. As a property of a model's own confidence it is the failure the archive keeps finding: chain-of-thought supervision reduces composition errors without fixing it, a model scores 52.4 percent when the correct option is A and 31.6 to 33.6 for other positions on a position-balanced set, and internal-state statistics differ sharply in calibration error under the same conditions (0.1216 and 0.0858 for one against 0.2451 and 0.1764 for another). As a property of a critic it has direct consequences for training, since a miscalibrated value estimate produces systematically asymmetric advantages -- one source tracks Brier 0.2031 to 0.1637 and expected calibration error 0.2612 to 0.2131, with the largest gap exactly where overestimation inflates negative advantages. As a fitted correction it inherits whatever defines its calibration set, and one source notes it needs paired truthful and hallucinated representations with no sensitivity analysis over set size or composition reported. Two methodological findings are transferable. The measurement is often achievable from the first 12 of 28 layers, so no full forward pass is needed. And a calibration improvement is only evidence if the metric and the objective are different measurements -- one archived method optimises answer-token confidence and is scored by an error computed from the same quantity, which makes the improvement partly a check that optimisation succeeded. One source names the design squeeze directly: models weak enough to have headroom lack the signal, and models with the signal lack the headroom, so the space where self-correction of calibration can be measured cleanly is nearly empty.
entropy collapse16The fall of policy entropy to near zero within a few hundred steps of reinforcement learning on reasoning tasks, and across 16 sources one of the archive's best-quantified training phenomena. It is front-loaded: across 11 runs of 2,400 gradient steps, 73 percent of entropy consumption and 76 percent of performance gain occur in the first 200 steps and the first 800 account for over 93 percent of gain with 94 percent of entropy loss, so more than two-thirds of training yields marginal returns. It coincides with the highest training reward, which several sources read as premature exploitation of a narrow set of high-reward patterns. The interpretive question the corpus takes seriously is what collapsed. A measured fall is compatible with the policy having calcified merely stylistic choices or with its having pruned genuinely distinct inferential pathways, and the source that separates them finds the collapse significantly stronger in a semantic contrast than a syntactic one -- so it is inferences rather than phrasing. That same source shows the compensating gain and the cost in one measurement: the trained policy's entropy collapses correctly where only one continuation is legal, and with illegal continuations masked away the flatter distilled policy outperforms it by about 60 percent. Many mitigations exist -- clipping variants, covariance-based penalties, entropy regularisation, strict on-policy updating, and restricting updates to high-entropy tokens -- and the corpus's caution about them is that one paper's largest reported share of the gain came from switching to strict on-policy training rather than from the entropy mechanism it credits, and that one theoretical account depends on the advantage of tokens never sampled, which most algorithms cannot estimate.
latent reasoning16Keeping intermediate computation in continuous hidden states instead of emitting tokens, which removes the token cost of a chain of thought and, by construction, removes the readable trace with it. Across 16 sources the corpus reports both sides of that trade and one uncomfortable null. The gains are real where the ablation is controlled: one source's continued-training control -- same checkpoint, same epochs, no latent replacement -- costs 0.65 to 1.63 mean F1 at three scales, so the effect is the latent mechanism rather than extra training, and another cuts 268.56 rationale tokens to 1.60 with an 8.9-fold speedup. Effort scales monotonically in one system (21, 27, 29.5 percent at low, medium and high) though its own minimum-effort comparison is statistically unresolved. The null is the sharpest result: on off-the-shelf models, continuous-state decoding is functionally indistinguishable from discrete decoding -- layer-wise entropy profiles coincide, KL between the two states reaches about 1e-4 in middle layers, cosine similarity 0.996 to 0.998 -- so a latent method that is not trained for it may be doing nothing latent at all. Two design findings recur: latent budgets are usually fixed per question when what a question needs varies, and reasoning latents without rationale supervision are worse than no reasoning latents at all. The inspectability cost is named directly by the one source applying it where decisions must remain auditable.
process reward15Scoring intermediate steps rather than only the final answer, so that a long trajectory carries signal at the points where credit is ambiguous. Across 15 sources the recurring finding is that it works only as a modifier on the terminal check: removing the sequence-level quality reward is the single largest degradation in one system while removing the process reward costs less than either that or removing reinforcement learning entirely, and pushing the process weight too high drives performance below the baseline it was meant to improve. Designs accordingly constrain it -- gating partial agreement with a reference so it cannot override a wrong final answer, applying the turn-level term multiplicatively so it reweights the trajectory advantage's magnitude while preserving its sign, or giving distinct trajectory segments separate advantage terms so an update improving one is not cancelled by the other sharing one scalar. The sources disagree about where the signal should come from and each choice carries its own defect. A learned verifier is informative and expensive -- the sparse-supervision trap one source names. A frozen reference policy's teacher-forced likelihood of a gold answer is cheap but exists only where a gold target does, so nothing transfers to inference or to reference-free settings, and it inherits the references' domain bias. Reusing step labels that process-annotation datasets already contain is cheapest of all, but the reward then measures agreement with one validated derivation, so a rollout reaching the verified answer through different intermediate quantities is penalised for being different rather than wrong -- and coverage is bounded by the annotations the premise says others pay to create. Hand-written criteria check surface quality of a trace rather than grounding, so a fluent well-structured passage unsupported by the evidence is rewarded. Ablations that remove it typically cost a few points (65.15 and 65.24 against a full method's 67.94 in one), which is the scale of contribution the archive should expect.
prompt difficulty15How hard a problem is for a given policy, and across 15 sources the variable that both training and inference allocation turn on. Its central property is that it is policy-relative and moving: a prompt is easy when every rollout succeeds and hard when none does, and both cases produce zero group-relative advantage, so the useful band is the middle -- one method shifts 82.7 percent of its gradient budget into the 0.2-0.9 pass-rate band against a baseline's 58.9. It also moves during training, which is why one source tracks per-task reward trajectories across 20 epochs and classifies tasks into six profiles including forgetting and stubbornly-unlearned rather than treating difficulty as a static label. At inference the same variable governs allocation, with several methods conditioning the thinking budget on it, and one finding the saving concentrated on the easy tail -- 53 to 81 percent token reduction for the top decile and percentile. Two cautions the archive attaches. Difficulty defined by a baseline's own pass rate selects for that baseline's failures, so part of any tiered improvement is regression toward the mean. And benchmark difficulty is only an approximation of what a particular policy can answer, which is why the better sources report tiers by measured pass rate rather than by benchmark label.
reasoning redundancy15The part of a chain of thought that does no work, and the quantity every efficiency method in this archive is trying to identify. Fifteen sources locate it differently — after the answer is derived, where double-checking continues; in tokens with negative marginal log-probability contribution to the correct answer; in segments the model's own likelihood landscape marks as extraneous; in the low-entropy convergence region after a sharp two-phase transition; in review nodes of a dependency graph that have too few descendants or sit too late; in steps receiving little attention from the reasoning-termination token; in later alternative solutions, argued to be actively harmful rather than merely wasteful; and in structure inherited from a teacher whose capacity did not match the student's. This note previously recorded that no source compared these criteria on the same trace. One now does, and the answer reframes the disagreement rather than settling it: at step granularity three importance criteria overlap 70-80% on which steps to preserve while diverging on which to delete, so the criteria converge on a shared reasoning backbone and differ only over interchangeable filler; at token granularity the agreement collapses, and only symbol-aware scoring avoids deleting operators and numbers. That study also refutes the premise several archived methods rest on, reporting that pruning which deliberately targets reflective statements performs no better than pruning that ignores them, because redundancy in long traces is diffuse — the skeleton is repeated and rephrased throughout rather than concentrated in a nameable class of step. Two caveats keep the question open: the comparison covers three generic scoring functions in a distillation setting, so the reasoning-specific criteria above are still untested against each other, and the 70-80% figure is a light-compression number that falls by half at aggressive ratios. Reported reductions run from roughly 40% to 87%, sometimes with accuracy gains.
localization14Attributing a behaviour to a specific part of a model — a layer, a head, a neuron, a direction, a parameter region — and the organizing question of this archive's interpretability work at fourteen sources. The sources agree it is possible and disagree about what a located component means. Granularity changes the answer: on propositional logic, four families of attention heads execute a sequential circuit, while on arithmetic the mechanism is an unordered bag of heuristic neurons, and no source tests whether a computation modular at head level is heuristic inside each head. Method choices change the answer too — how prompts are corrupted, which metric scores the effect and whether layers are patched singly or in windows all shift what activation patching reports, and single-component tracing cannot see components that matter only jointly. Two cautions recur. Being encoded is not being used: a concept can be linearly recoverable while having no influence on the output, and sparse autoencoders improve the first while attenuating the second. And what is located may be a state rather than a property, since memorizing and generalizing circuits compete during training.
process supervision14Supervising or verifying intermediate steps rather than only the final answer, across 14 sources both a method family and an assumption the archive questions. The assumption is that a written scratchpad exposes the intermediate variables that matter for later behaviour -- and the faithfulness literature says written reasoning can be incomplete, post-hoc, or driven by features it never mentions, so a step label may be scoring a description rather than a computation. The cost objection is the other standing one: step-level verification isolates errors precisely and is computationally exhaustive, usually requiring either annotation or a separately trained verifier. The archived methods respond by finding a cheaper source of step signal -- reusing labels that existing datasets already contain, deriving them from a frozen reference policy's likelihood of a gold answer, distilling them from a teacher that can see the whole trajectory, or computing them from the divergence between two context views of the same model. All of these buy density at the cost of measuring agreement with something rather than correctness, which is the trade the archive's process-reward entry records in detail.
reward hacking14Satisfying a reward without doing the thing it was meant to measure, and across 14 sources the failure that recurs whenever a reward term is not gated on the answer being correct. The archive's clearest measured instances all take that shape: an unconstrained length reward is exploited to the context limit with macro-F1 collapsing to 0.210; a bounded target-constrained penalty buys about 990 tokens of coherent but functionally useless filler at essentially the collapsed score; a reward for reflection invites reflective text that scores well without changing what the agent does; and on subjective judgement tasks mean completion length falls from about 300 tokens to 170 after roughly 70 steps because guessing the label earns the same reward more cheaply. Only the version paying the bonus when the answer is correct converts the behaviour into accuracy. A second family concerns the measuring instrument rather than the objective: judge-based rewards and judge-scored benchmarks are themselves hackable, and one source notes the inverse problem -- a model that does reason can be classified as not reasoning and rewarded incorrectly. That source reports the residual rate below 10 percent and, as the archive notes, does not state how the hacking was detected for that measurement, which is the general difficulty: a reward hack is defined by a gap between the reward and the intent, and measuring the gap requires an instrument outside the reward.
benchmark contamination12Test items appearing in training data and inflating benchmark scores, and across 12 sources a problem the archive treats as mostly unmeasurable in the field. Four findings shape that view. Detection is fragile to the training pipeline: contamination introduced at the supervised stage is identifiable, and a brief reinforcement-learning run markedly suppresses the signals detectors rely on -- attributed to importance sampling and clipping rather than to any specific algorithm, so a broad class of post-training launders what preceded it. A negative audit is ambiguous: a detector returning no evidence has said either that the benchmark is clean or that the audit had too little power, and nothing in the literature separates them. Exposure mechanism matters: verbatim, paraphrase and surface-shuffle exposure produce sharply different behavioural signals, so a result established under one does not cover the others, and simulated contamination by mixing test items into a fine-tuning corpus produces verbatim-style exposure specifically. And mitigation metrics can be gamed by damage: one aggregate scores a synthetic strategy that makes the contaminated model fail every question better than no intervention at all, because over-suppression cancels against residual contamination -- a stratified reading ranks the same strategy worst. The practical instruments the corpus offers are a leakage ladder (question-only, empty, random, shuffled, matched, full-solution answer-recovery rates) and similarity audits reported with their false positives inspected by hand; both establish implausibility rather than absence.
exploration-exploitation trade-off12The tension this archive's entropy literature is organized around: a policy that concentrates probability mass gains reward on patterns it already has and stops discovering new ones. Twelve sources make it measurable rather than rhetorical, and they disagree about what to measure. The entropy family reads collapse as premature exploitation, supported by plain GRPO reaching the highest training reward at the lowest entropy, and takes pass@k at large k rather than accuracy as the metric that separates the two — which is also what supports the claim that RL sharpens sampling inside the base model's reachable set. Several sources then argue entropy is the wrong handle, replacing it with distributional deviation from the group average, with a diversity bonus over hidden-state representations that removes the pass@k degradation entropy methods leave behind, or with a per-token discriminator's deviation from a policy-weighted baseline. Two entries move the trade-off out of training entirely and pose it as a stopping problem — how many samples to draw before quitting — with classical optimal-stopping theory supplying the rule. A theoretical entry states the cost most sharply: exploration bought with an entropy bonus is paid for permanently in the location of the optimum unless the coefficient is annealed away.
post-hoc rationalization12Producing an answer first and then a chain that justifies it, and across 12 sources both a failure mode the archive measures and a training practice several papers adopt deliberately. As a failure it is quantified: under injected bias, accuracy drops by as much as 36 percent across 13 tasks with the explanations rationalising the biased answers, and under cache compression the answer-first signature rises from 58 percent of failure cases to 94. That second source contains the archive's sharpest statement of the cost -- across compressors, accuracy and reasoning quality correlate at Spearman -0.95, so an accuracy-based leaderboard would order methods almost exactly backwards on chain validity. As a practice it appears wherever supervision is manufactured from known answers: rule-generated chains reverse-engineered from oracle allocations, and reasoning traces generated with the answer in hand and admitted only when they reach it. Both report downstream gains including out of distribution, which is the honest reading -- training on rationalised traces can improve behaviour -- but the traces are not accounts of any inference, and a paper claiming reduced fabrication from such a corpus has to contend with the alternative that its traces were selected for agreeing with the label.
out-of-distribution generalization11Whether a trained improvement survives a change in the input distribution, and across 11 sources the axis on which the archive's supervision results most often separate. Two patterns recur. Rationale supervision is worth little in domain and a great deal out of it -- 20 to 28 points across four models on a contamination-audited external benchmark, against nothing measurable in domain for strong backbones -- and a controlled human study attributes the measurable effect to language adherence and inspectability rather than to better reasoning. And reasoning helps most where the recovery is needed: one self-correction method's out-of-domain gains reach 10.9 and 8.8 points where its in-domain gains average about one, and removing the reasoning step costs 8.7 percent in distribution and 55.6 percent out. The corpus's caution is that out-of-distribution is a direction, not a place: transfer to three related languages is near-identical in average between two methods while distributed differently across the three; a defect direction fitted on controlled pairs scores 83.3 percent held out on those pairs and 19.6 to 30.0 on real benchmarks; and a correctness predictor falls from 0.87 to 0.60 across three shifts while the structural geometry it was computed from transfers intact.
privileged information11Giving a training-time teacher an input the deployed student will never have -- a reference solution, a completed trajectory with its verified outcome, a skill card, an evidence set -- so that the shift it induces in the teacher's distribution can be distilled into the student. Across 11 sources the framework is stable and the design question is entirely what the privileged field should contain. The corpus's answers disagree usefully: the golden answer specifies the target rather than the evidence, a reusable skill carries no answer at all but also no query-specific information, and a completed trajectory carries hindsight about this student on this problem. Two constraints recur. The field must be matched rather than merely present, which is why these papers run scrambling controls -- and the ordering of removal against scrambling is itself the measurement, since a mismatched field worse than none means the method depends on correspondence while a mismatched field better than none means most of the benefit is marginal. And a formal limit applies: forward-KL distillation projects a trajectory-conditioned teacher onto its conditional mean given the prefix, so the portion of a privileged teacher's advantage that depends on information the student will not have is untransferable in principle. The corpus's own scope note is blunt -- several sources list requiring a privileged teacher as their limitation -- and one finds that a self-teacher too weak to perform the required analysis recovers almost none of the gain.
reasoning trajectory11A reasoning trace read as a path -- through hidden-state space, through answer space, or through a sequence of committed states -- rather than as a set of independent steps. Across 11 sources it is the framing behind a cluster of methods that share one argument: static activations let probes latch onto lexical surface patterns, so layer-to-layer or step-to-step displacement is the more reliable signal. The archive's results from that framing: error detection from residual-stream motion combined with a region reader and a direction reader; verification without training by comparing a trace's start-to-end hidden-state delta against class centroids; correctness read from step-specific geometry that transfers across tasks while the correctness predictor computed from it does not; and two-dimensional projections of distance-to-answer-choice features that make visible how incorrect trajectories converge earlier than correct ones. Two failure modes are located on the trajectory rather than in it: the sentences that commit a model to agreeing with an incorrect user suggestion, and the step at which a model that correctly flagged a query as harmful overrides that judgement. The corpus's caution is that trajectory geometry's advantage over an ordinary probe on the final state is within a few thousandths of AUROC on several benchmarks, so the general claim is carried by a minority of them.
answer stabilization10How much a reasoning algorithm's answer has stopped changing, and across 10 sources the signal several archived systems use to decide when to stop spending. Its clearest formulation is algorithm-agnostic and built into a serving system, saving up to 50 percent of tokens in batch inference and tripling online throughput by reallocating or terminating per query. Two findings complicate the simple reading that convergence means correctness. A model's first solution is usually its best and later alternatives are actively harmful in one study, which structures the errors as a forest and prunes accordingly -- so continued generation past stabilisation is not neutral. And double-checking after the correct answer has already been derived is identifiable as a distinct portion of a trace and targetable by a compression reward. Against those, the archive's calibration material bounds what stabilisation licenses: uniformly high confidence across a trace indicates a failure to branch rather than a well-supported answer, and in the highest-agreement bin the plurality is correct about half the time on hard problems. One source finds a model family badly miscalibrated yet ranking correct from incorrect well, because its confidence tracks structural consistency rather than correctness -- which is stabilisation and correctness coming apart explicitly.
exploration10Whether training discovers trajectories the policy would not already produce, and across 10 sources a quantity the archive treats as poorly measured rather than well understood. The corpus's central methodological point is that the usual proxies do not measure it: token entropy cannot distinguish exploration from learning wrong trajectories -- one source calls a high-entropy condition 'entropy without exploration' -- and pass@k describes inference-time behaviour after training rather than what training discovered. The replacement several sources converge on is a paired count against a reference run: a prompt is rescued if the method produces a correct rollout where the baseline produced none, and lost in the opposite case, with degenerate and incorrect rollouts counted alongside. Its authors are explicit about the limits -- defined relative to a baseline it is a comparative diagnostic rather than an absolute measure, and it counts a rescue on one correct rollout without asking whether that rollout's reasoning was sound. The substantive findings are that verifiable-reward training reduces exploration on both modalities measured, though the controlled comparison is against a distilled policy trained on the same data rather than against the base model, and that concentrating exploration has a real if small cost on shorter horizons -- one transfer result loses on the benchmark where its host method was strongest.
hallucination10Producing content unsupported by the evidence, and across 10 sources a category the archive's better work refuses to treat as one thing. The most useful decomposition separates reasoning hallucination, where a flawed inference step propagates into a wrong conclusion and the model could in principle have got it right, from knowledge hallucination, where the model lacks the facts and no amount of further reasoning supplies them -- worse, repeated refinement can make an unsupported answer more convincing. That paper's ablation shows the two remedies are not interchangeable: reflection alone leaves coverage at exactly 100 percent while abstention alone leaves the hallucination proxy essentially unchanged at 20.0, because refusing on unanswerable prompts does nothing about errors on answerable ones. A second decomposition, in vision, splits by whether the failure survives masking the attended region -- masking works for the uncertainty type and is useless for the prior type by construction. Two measurement lessons recur. A hallucination number must be read with coverage: one system's 28.5-point reduction in its proxy costs 9.6 points of coverage, and its reflection-depth sweep is monotone in opposite directions on the two axes, which the authors use to justify keeping reflection shallow rather than to claim a best setting. And a mitigation that reduces mentions may be suppressing output rather than grounding it, which is why the stronger sources report word count, correct-object coverage and perplexity alongside the reduction.
meta-evaluation10Evaluating the evaluation — asking whether a benchmark, metric or judge measures what it claims. Ten sources practise it, and the recurring result is that the validation layer is weaker than the thing it validates. Exact-match agreement with human labels, the standard way to certify an LLM judge, is shown insufficient over roughly 541,000 judgments; forcing annotators to pick one answer on tasks admitting several defensible readings biases that validation badly; and judge preferences track style rather than the properties they are supposed to measure. Benchmarks fare no better under the same scrutiny: decomposing a task by cognitive dimension, ablating the modality the task supposedly requires, or scoring intermediate artefacts separately each reveal that the headline number was carrying something else. The archive also holds a call to apply this to interpretability itself, after two papers reached opposite conclusions on one behaviour and a third found both partly right and incomparable.
effective depth9How much of a network's depth a computation actually uses, and across 9 sources a quantity that appears in three different literatures with the same shape. Theoretically it is the thing chain of thought buys: a bounded-depth transformer is limited by its layers in one forward pass, and emitting intermediate tokens converts sequence length into effective depth, which is why the number of steps maps onto complexity classes. Empirically it appears as depth-localisation -- grounding detection climbing through early layers, peaking mid-network and falling toward the output; the useful read-out band being narrow and mid-network; and reasoning-relevant signals concentrated in particular layers rather than spread. And as a mechanism it appears in the counting work, where latent counts are stored, transferred through dedicated heads and aggregated at the final stage, which is a depth-structured computation rather than a distributed one. The archive's practical consequence is that pooling across depth dilutes rather than aggregates, and that a claim about where a computation lives needs the layer sweep rather than a single site.
monosemanticity9Whether a unit responds to one interpretable thing or to several unrelated ones -- the property a feature decomposition is trying to recover and the property individual neurons lack. Across 9 sources it functions as the motivating premise for dictionary learning: reverse engineering requires units analysable in isolation, neurons activate across semantically unrelated contexts, and for the residual stream there is no reason to expect features to align with the neuron basis at all. The archive's evidence about how well it is achieved is mixed and quantitative: automated interpretability scores for sparse coding run about 0.30 at the first layer and decline to 0.15 by the fifth, against 0.14 for an independent-components baseline and 0.06 to 0.08 for random directions -- so the improvement over chance is real and shrinks with depth. The standing caution is that a monosemantic-looking feature is a property of a particular dictionary rather than of the model, since feature splitting and absorption move the boundaries between features as the dictionary changes.
policy entropy8The entropy of a policy's next-token distribution, and across 8 sources the quantity whose trajectory is the archive's best-instrumented view of what reinforcement learning does to a reasoning model. Its behaviour is consistent: collapse to near zero within a few hundred steps, coinciding with the highest training reward, after which the policy is overly confident, cannot explore, and its validation performance plateaus. The archive's related entry records the front-loading -- roughly three-quarters of both entropy consumption and performance gain in the first 200 of 2,400 steps. Two cautions the corpus supplies about using it as a signal. It does not measure exploration: high entropy can be degeneration, and one source calls a condition it examines 'entropy without exploration' outright, which is why a paired rescue-and-loss count against a reference run is the replacement several sources adopt. And it is a summary over a distribution, so it cannot distinguish stylistic from inferential narrowing -- the branch-preference measurement that can find the collapse stronger in a semantic contrast than a syntactic one. Where it is used well it is as a control rather than a target: one source reports its post-initialisation entropy at 0.047 against a baseline's 0.029 precisely to show its improvement did not come from collapsing diversity.
reasoning boundary8The set of problems a model can solve at all given enough attempts, as distinct from how reliably it reaches them -- operationalised as pass@k at large k. Across 8 sources it is the axis on which the archive's central disagreement about reinforcement learning is fought, and the corpus's contribution is that the disagreement is a measurement artefact rather than a difference of opinion. Under plain pass@k the base model overtakes at large k and the boundary appears to narrow, measured directly: pass@1 rises across training steps while coverage at pass@256 falls, and in one case a trained policy's pass@256 sits below its untrained base. Under CoT-Pass@K, which scores the chain as well as the answer, the trained model leads at every k, because base models win plain pass@k partly by producing wrong chains that land on right answers. The reconciliation the archive holds is that both are true of different sets: valid-but-different paths are pruned, which costs plain coverage, and invalid paths are pruned harder, which is what the chain-scored metric sees. Two extensions matter. The narrowing is not specific to reward-based training -- on-policy distillation from a stronger teacher shows the same crossing with no reward involved, which places the effect in on-policy training on self-generated trajectories. And a method can widen it: one exploration method's pass@k is competitive with both its baseline and the base model uniformly across k, which is the property to ask for rather than a large-k win alone.
memorization7Reproducing training content rather than computing an answer, and across 7 sources a hypothesis the archive tests rather than assumes. Its cleanest separation is the loose-versus-strict metric under symbolic variabilisation: a base mathematics model loses 38.5 percent even under partial credit, consistent with heavy reliance on memorised patterns, while reinforcement-trained models lose 2.6 to 7.2 -- so the two metrics together separate memorisation from mere inconsistency. A mechanistic source rules it out for arithmetic specifically, finding neither a learned algorithm nor memorisation but an unordered collection of sparse heuristic neurons voting. And a benchmark source finds the opposite asymmetry for proverbs, where completion is easy and choice is hard. The archive's related material covers the unlearning side and the contamination-detection side, where the standing result is that post-training suppresses the signals memorisation detectors rely on.
multimodal reasoning7Reasoning that must combine visual and textual evidence, and across 7 sources a setting where the archive's recurring finding is that answer accuracy conceals whether the image was used at all. Three measurements make that concrete. A visual-aid benchmark finds high answer accuracy hiding near-total failure at producing or reasoning from the aids the problems require. A university-level mathematics benchmark with original, hand-drawn, photographed and text-only variants finds a model given no image beating its own multimodal variants and a frontier system. And test-time selection fails for a structural reason -- neither answer frequency nor verbalised confidence tests whether an answer depends on the pixels, so a grounded answer and a confident language-prior guess look identical at the selection layer. Two failure modes specific to the modality: a model can see a risky visual cue and let narrative coherence override it as reasoning proceeds, and explicit intermediate grounding is worth 8.78 points over letting the model attend implicitly. The corpus's general verdict is that leading proprietary and open models remain significantly limited on complex multimodal reasoning, and that the reasoning process itself introduces vulnerabilities the single-modality case does not have.
process evaluation7Scoring the reasoning rather than the answer, and across 7 sources the evaluation the archive keeps finding is necessary and rarely done. Its measured motivation: high answer accuracy conceals near-total failure at producing or reasoning from required visual aids; a benchmark of mathematical proof reveals failure modes an answer check cannot see; and error-detection benchmarks show frontier models about 10 points behind human evaluation at saying where a chain went wrong. Two construction approaches appear -- symbolic generation so that every intermediate quantity is verifiable by construction, and expert annotation of the specific span that establishes a conclusion, which one source uses to show that predicting the right answer and giving the right reason are separable abilities. The archive's standing caution is that a step score computed by a model inherits that model's reliability, and that agreement with one reference derivation penalises a different valid one.
reproducibility7Whether a reported result can be obtained again, and across 7 sources a property the archive treats as scarce enough to be worth measuring. Its instances divide by what fails to reproduce. Method-level: an independent reproduction matches a published grid to two decimal places and then finds the advantage vanishing on every new domain, with no per-item difference reaching significance across four of them. Instrument-level: if two runs on the same activations give different dictionaries, a claim about a feature is a claim about a run -- which is the sparse-autoencoder identifiability problem stated as a reproducibility one. Protocol-level: measured performance depends on template, decoder, controller, reducer, verifier, stopping rule and numerical settings, so accuracy reported without the inference protocol is not reproducible in principle; one source prescribes declaring budget, temperature, evidence map, stopping and extraction rules before outcomes are inspected. Numerical-level: one source studies sources of nondeterminism in inference itself. The archive's practice, where sources model it well, is byte-identical prompts verified by hash, fixed seeds, and pre-registration of thresholds before the confirmatory analysis.

GRPO62Sampling a group of rollouts per prompt, standardising their rewards within the group to form the advantage, and optimising a PPO-style clipped surrogate with a KL penalty -- so no value network is learned. Across 62 sources it is less a method than the substrate everything else is built on ('the implementation is a few lines on top of GRPO'), and its properties are consequences of the group normalisation rather than of the reward. Four are documented repeatedly. Prompt weighting: for binary rewards the induced weight is one over the square root of p(1-p), which diverges at both ends, and the realised consequence has been measured -- 36.4 percent of one benchmark's token-level gradient budget spent on prompts already above 0.9 pass rate. Silent groups: a prompt whose rollouts all agree has zero advantage for every member and contributes nothing, reported at 63 to 68 percent of training groups in one study and rising across epochs in another as the reward saturates. Relative spacing only: what reaches the optimiser is the ordering and gaps of rewards within the group, so a linear rescaling is cancelled while a bounded nonlinear one is not, an unbounded reward lets one outlier inflate the group standard deviation and flatten every near-optimal sibling, and a difficulty-dependent reward multiplier interacts badly with the normalisation -- weakening a token penalty precisely where outcome variance is highest. Entropy: policy entropy falls from about 0.25 to near zero within roughly 50 steps, with the highest training reward coinciding with the lowest entropy, which several sources read as premature exploitation; a large family of clipping and covariance variants exists to slow it, and one source finds switching from approximate to strict on-policy updating worth 4.6 and 5.3 points, more than the entropy method layered on top. On what it does to reasoning the sources split. One gives a theoretical account in which the gradient raises the probability of correct chains even though the reward sees only the answer, once pretraining supplies priors separating them; another finds accuracy improving without the traces becoming more semantically valid, which it calls unsurprising for an outcome reward. Two failure modes recur: on subjective judgement tasks mean completion length collapses from about 300 tokens to 170 after roughly 70 steps because guessing the label earns the same reward more cheaply, and pass@1 rises across training steps while coverage at pass@256 falls, so the reachable set narrows as reliability improves. A brief run of it also suppresses the signals benchmark-contamination detectors rely on, which one source attributes to PPO-style importance sampling and clipping rather than to anything specific here. Practical cautions from the corpus: it is dominated in cost by rollout generation, and adaptive allocators beat full-group training substantially under a matched token budget; against PPO under an identical reward it wins 81.2 to 72.6 where correctness is localised to a few tokens, which the authors attribute to group-relative rather than batch-level advantage normalisation; and it is frequently an under-tuned baseline, gaining under 4 points from best-checkpoint selection where a supervised baseline gains 27, and in one case scoring below its own zero-shot starting point on a benchmark. Reported margins over it are commonly 0.75 to 2.6 points with no seeds or variance.
supervised fine-tuning54Maximising the likelihood of curated target sequences -- usually a reasoning trace followed by an answer -- and across 54 sources the stage whose role is most often asserted and least often measured. Its dominant use here is as a cold start that installs a format before reinforcement learning, and where that has been ablated the effect is categorical rather than incremental: a tool-using policy trained by reinforcement learning alone cannot reliably emit parsable tool calls at all (50.6 percent format compliance against 100), while supervised fine-tuning alone reaches 99.8 percent and comes within 2 to 3.6 points of the full pipeline. The sources disagree sharply about whether it is needed. Several find it indispensable and then surpassed -- it beats the base model and loses to every reinforcement-learning method applied over it, or plateaus at 89.74 percent where the full pipeline reaches higher. Others find the opposite: reinforcement learning without any cold start is the best configuration on closed-ended medical questions on all three benchmarks by 8 to 20 points while losing every open-ended split, one hybrid-reasoning method removes the stage deliberately as computationally costly, and one from-scratch study finds group-relative reinforcement learning losing to supervised fine-tuning on two of three mathematics benchmarks (27.37 against 36.69 Pass@8 on one) with the combination beating both by 31 to 175 percent. Two systematic reasons the comparison keeps coming out differently are documented. It is routinely under-tuned as a baseline: giving each method its best validation checkpoint rather than a fixed epoch budget is worth 27 points of end-to-end accuracy to supervised fine-tuning and under 4 to reinforcement learning, which reverses the reported ordering. And the comparison usually confounds the training method with the trace source, since the supervised arm typically learns from a larger distilled teacher while the reinforcement-learning arm has none. On what the traces themselves buy, two controlled studies agree that the effect is out of distribution rather than in it: rationale supervision buys nothing in-domain for strong backbones and 20 to 28 points out of domain, and domain-specific chain-of-thought supervision lifts one 9B model from 37.4 to 89.0 percent where answer-only supervision reaches 75.1. Quantities are small throughout -- 1,000 curated traces for test-time scaling, 1,000 examples for a safety-alignment structure change, 800 to install or destroy a habit of verbalising uncertainty (suppressing it halves pass@1 on five models despite every training trace being correct). Two sources note where the stage is dangerous rather than useful: a triggered backdoor whose visible reasoning stays clean and correct is installed this way, and benchmark contamination introduced here is laundered by a brief reinforcement-learning run afterwards, so a pipeline ending in reinforcement learning conceals what its supervised stage absorbed. One source places more alignment weight on this stage than the preference-optimisation literature assumes, identifying data scaling and prompt diversity as the drivers.
LLM-as-a-judge49Using a language model to score another model's output, and across 49 sources the archive's most pervasive dependency -- it appears as an evaluation metric, a training reward, a data filter, a contamination detector and an annotator of the archive's own probing labels. The corpus is largely a catalogue of what it gets wrong. Validity: judge preference scores do not correlate with concrete measures of safety, world knowledge or instruction following, because judges systematically prioritise style over factuality; judges also produce characteristic scores regardless of the text evaluated. Reliability without validity: one large-scale evaluation finds generational progress legible on one judging benchmark (0.309, 0.487, 0.606) and nearly invisible on another (0.451, 0.451, 0.457). Instrument dependence: a standard prompt turns out to be specialised to the model it was authored against, with every other model including that model's own newer sibling performing significantly worse under it, so a prompt is not a neutral instrument carried between judges. Disagreement concentrates where it matters: an independent audit of 400 responses finds 90.25 percent overall agreement between two judges and a 41.94 percent false-negative rate on the small subset the reference judge calls unsafe -- exactly the class that determines the reported score. As a reward it is expensive and fragile: one system issues a judge call per rubric per rollout per group per step, another finds a judge-based alternative costing 62 percent of per-step wall clock against 6 percent for a structural rule, and a third falls below its plain baseline when the judge is weakened. Two structural cautions recur across papers rather than within them. A judge drawn from the same family as one of the systems it scores is not an independent instrument, which several sources note about their own evaluations. And validating a judge against human annotation performed under the judge's own criteria does not establish independence -- one paper reports 95.6 percent agreement and says so explicitly. Where the corpus is positive it is about ensembling and validation rather than about judges as such: a jury of different models beats any member, and the practice several sources model is to report agreement, kappa and a hand inspection of disagreements before concluding anything from the labels.
linear probe32Fitting a linear classifier on hidden states to test whether a property is linearly decodable, and across 32 sources the archive's most-used and least-standardised instrument. What it reliably shows is that a great deal is decodable and often earlier than the model says it: probes recover 77 to 82 percent on a theory-of-mind task against the same models' own chain-of-thought outputs at 62 to 70, with the answer available already in middle layers; probes detect sycophantic anchors at 74 to 85 percent balanced accuracy above text-only baselines. Four cautions recur and are the reason the archive treats a probe result as a lower bound on a claim rather than the claim. Probes learn what is easiest: activations are saturated with polysemantic features, so a probe can succeed for surface lexical reasons rather than for the structure it was meant to find, which is why null and permutation controls appear in the better sources. Extraction choices dominate: no configuration of read-out location, pooling and framing is best across benchmarks, and a pooled variance decomposition can rank a factor below one percent because its opposite effects on two benchmarks cancel. Decoding licenses nothing about intervening: the difference-of-means family used for steering is sometimes the weakest of four classifiers on the same data, and one source declines to pick its intervention by classification accuracy for exactly that reason. And the margin over the obvious simpler control is often small -- on three of six benchmarks in one study the advantage of trajectory geometry over an ordinary probe on the final hidden state is within a few thousandths of AUROC, so a general claim rests on two benchmarks. Sources also note the linear restriction itself makes their reported correlations lower bounds.
chain-of-thought prompting31Eliciting step-by-step intermediate tokens from a model that would otherwise answer directly, usually by few-shot examples drawn from the dataset -- which across 31 sources remains the standard baseline in official model releases and in nearly every comparison the archive holds. Its status here is almost entirely that of a reference point rather than a subject: methods report 6.6 points over it averaged across 30 settings, or 74 percent against its 4 percent on a search-structured puzzle, and it is the arm every latent-reasoning, self-consistency and structured-prompting paper must beat. Two qualifications recur across the corpus. The prompt is an experimental variable rather than a fixed point, so a comparison against it measures the template as much as the method -- one source holds the template constant across arms precisely so that only the diversity source differs. And what it improves is the answer, not necessarily the reasoning: the archive's faithfulness sources find extrinsic prompting and supplied cues both helping while neither is internalised, leaving reasoning that proceeds disconnected from the evidence it claims to rest on. One source in the corpus explains its accuracy effect mechanistically; the others use it without one.
self-consistency28Sampling several chains and returning the answer they mostly agree on, and across 28 sources the baseline every test-time-scaling method must beat, the aggregation rule most of them replace, and increasingly the method the archive can say is unsafe on hard inputs. Three lines of evidence. It is beaten by reweighting rather than by more sampling: entropy-, instability- and geometry-based weightings all report margins that grow with the sample count rather than closing, and the mechanism offered is that incorrect trajectories converge to their wrong answer earlier than correct ones converge to the right one, so counting votes rewards the failure mode. It is not monotone: scaling it can amplify spurious consensus, with 64 rollouts reaching 34.42 against 34.66 at 16 in one label-free training setting, and on a hard science benchmark it lowers per-problem accuracy on 56.6 and 65.7 percent of problems for two small models while the aggregate barely moves. And it is a strong control that absorbs many proposed alternatives -- one study finds confidence-weighted variants tracking a matched control within noise, and a reproduction finds a published improvement never significantly beating it across four new domains, gaining 2.5 points at a larger budget on a set where only 2 of 80 items separate them, and losing the advantage entirely when the evaluation set is enlarged to 200. The precondition the corpus identifies is calibration: agreement must track correctness, and where the highest-agreement bin is right about half the time -- or less often than the lowest bin -- no verifier-free reweighting can repair it.
RLVR27Reinforcement learning against an automatic correctness check -- a test suite, an exact-match verifier, a deterministic evaluator -- and across 27 sources the dominant post-training method here and the one the archive has argued about most. The central dispute is whether it expands what a model can reach or only sharpens what it already could, and the corpus's contribution is that both are true of different sets: branch-preference entropy of a trained policy is below its distilled counterpart on 95.5 to 100 percent of sampled branches across four model families, with the collapse stronger for semantically distinct continuations than for syntactic variants, while backtracking probability at dead ends and recovery from injected distractors both rise sharply. The synthesis one source offers is that it buys sample efficiency by learning what is invalid and pays for it by pruning what is valid but different. Two scope conditions recur. It works where the pretraining corpus is dense in step-by-step derivations and a checker is clean; outside that -- subjective judgement, open-ended generation -- the same objective produces degeneration, with completion length collapsing because guessing earns the same reward more cheaply. And gains are brittle to how a question is worded: paraphrase and template change degrade them, traced to a binary verifier that cannot separate a wrong answer from a misformatted one. Its label-free variants replace the gold answer with the majority answer among rollouts, which inherits the failure modes of consensus. One further consequence the archive records: a brief run of it suppresses the signals benchmark-contamination detectors rely on.
activation steering21Adding a direction to a model's activations at inference to change its behaviour, and across 21 sources an instrument whose defaults do most of the damage attributed to the technique. Three defaults are named. The direction: choosing it by discriminability -- what the foundational method does -- can install the opposite of the intended effect, since vectors combining high separability with a strictly negative dose-response relationship are minable systematically. The site: injection layers are fixed globally per behaviour with which and how many treated as configuration, and one source shows the useful band is narrow, mid-network, and different for each model and each benchmark. The units: an apparent emergence of steerability with scale is produced entirely by raw activation units, an uncalibrated read-out metric and a fixed hand-picked operating point, and a factorial decomposition finds no significant trend in any of four cells. What the technique does establish, where controls are run, is causal direction rather than magnitude -- reducing steering strength consistently reduces reasoning token count and accuracy while leaving answer length unaffected, which is the shape of evidence the archive treats as usable. Two standing requirements follow from the corpus: report a matched null of comparable magnitude, since steering is an off-distribution intervention at a conventionally chosen strength and a degradation under it can be damage rather than mechanism; and do not read a probe's accuracy as a prediction about what steering along the same direction will do.
activation patching19Replacing an activation at a chosen site with the activation a different input produced, and measuring what the output does -- the standard localisation tool in mechanistic interpretability. Across 19 sources its recurring problem is not the idea but the absence of a convention: the literature contains many variants with no consensus on hyperparameters, each paper using its own corruption method and its own evaluation metric, so two patching results are frequently not comparable. One theoretical source sharpens this into a claim about what is measured: patching and weight-space ablation measure two different quantities -- a carrier's donor-receiver contrast versus its absolute level at the receiver -- and neither bounds the other, with an exact criterion for when ablating a subset collapses a conditional onto one branch. The archive's practical requirements follow. A patch needs a control pair rather than a single corruption, since a change under an arbitrary substitute shows that something moved. And where a compact subspace suffices -- replacing the whole residual stream at a site having a comparable effect to a low-rank patch -- that is the informative result, because it bounds where the causal information sits rather than merely confirming it is somewhere upstream.
reward shaping19Adding terms to a reward beyond the thing actually being verified -- length, format, efficiency, safety proximity, confidence, agreement with a reference derivation -- and across 19 sources the design decision that most often decides whether a training run works or degenerates. Three findings recur. A shaping term is a modifier on the terminal check, never a replacement: removing the sequence-level quality reward is the single largest degradation in one translation system (in-domain BLEU 29.83 to 22.94), removing the task-completion reward collapses another from 95.14 to 29.92 percent whole-response accuracy while decision and tool-selection accuracy stay at 100 -- the agent picks correctly at every step and never stops -- and removing the terminal outcome reward is the only ablation degrading every benchmark in a third. Anything not gated on correctness is satisfied vacuously: an unconstrained length reward is exploited to the 8192-token limit with macro-F1 collapsing to 0.210, a bounded target-constrained penalty buys about 990 tokens of coherent but functionally useless filler at 0.760 -- essentially the collapsed level -- and only the form paying the bonus when the answer is correct converts the preserved length into accuracy at 0.851. And the shape matters as much as the terms, because group normalisation preserves nonlinear reshaping of within-group reward spacing but cancels linear rescaling: a bounded reciprocal mapping of a continuous error gap beats the naive unbounded one and beats four published changes to the optimisation procedure on the same task, while a difficulty-dependent multiplier interacts badly with the normalisation and weakens a penalty exactly where outcome variance is highest. Two further cautions from the corpus: a hybrid hard-constraint-plus-continuous-soft scheme beats every static tolerance by 11.63 points because a single threshold cannot both be strict enough to be meaningful and dense enough to give gradient; and several sources set their weighting coefficients to 1.0 explicitly to avoid task-specific tuning, which is honest and leaves no sensitivity analysis behind.
best-of-n18Sampling N complete candidates and returning the one a verifier or reward model ranks highest. Across 18 sources it is the standard selection baseline and the standard illustration of two structural problems. It can decay with scale: with an imperfect verifier, adversarial solutions that fool the verifier become more likely to be found as N grows, so there is no guarantee that more samples help. And it fixes N in advance, wasting compute when an acceptable output arrives early and underspending when the prompt is hard -- one adaptive variant reaches the same reward with 15 to 35 percent fewer generations, and a compute-optimal strategy reports more than a fourfold efficiency improvement over it. Two observations from the corpus are worth keeping when reading reward-model papers. The ranking of process reward models for selection here does not carry over to their use as policy models: in one comparison only the weakest selection variant improved policy performance, a disconnect the source reports without explanation even though the two uses are what such a model is for. And the cost accounting matters -- one system's best-of-16 leads a cheaper method by 0.70 points at 2.43 times the parameter-weighted cost, with the paired interval straddling zero.
majority voting18Returning the answer most sampled responses agree on, and across 18 sources the aggregation baseline everything is measured against and almost everything beats. Its failure has a theoretical statement and an empirical one. Theoretically it converges to zero whenever some incorrect answer is individually more probable than the correct one, so more samples make it worse rather than better in that regime. Empirically that regime is reachable: on a hard graduate science benchmark it lowers per-problem accuracy on 56.6 and 65.7 percent of problems for two models while barely moving the aggregate, and in one test-time-training setting it actively lowers average accuracy (36.92 to 35.09) by converging toward an incorrect consensus. It is also beaten by every reweighting scheme the archive holds, typically by margins that grow with the sample count -- 60.6 percent against its 46.2 at sixteen samples in one comparison, and 51.7 and 50.9 for two confidence-based selectors in the same table. Used as a training signal rather than a selector, its characteristic damage is localised: one source's KL decomposition shows divergence on answer tokens rising sharply under it while reasoning-token divergence grows slowly, and masking the answer span keeps the former low and stable. The archive's summary position is that it is the right null and the wrong method: it assumes agreement tracks correctness, and the calibration measurements show that on hard problems it does not.
knowledge distillation15Training a student on a teacher's outputs or distribution, and across 15 sources a method whose success is governed less by the objective than by three properties of the pairing. Capacity floor: the same jury distillation that lifts a 12B student above every open reasoning-model judge lowers a 4B one (system-level 86.50 to 82.48), a limit the source scopes around rather than states. Idiom match: a safety-alignment pipeline gains across one model family and loses utility across another at small scale, which the authors attribute to a reasoning-style mismatch with the teacher's family, and a language-specific 9B model gains least from further rationale distillation while the weakest general backbone gains most. Mismatch regime: one method's advantage over standard on-policy distillation is 1.0 point where the student is close to the teacher -- inside the spread of three other baselines -- and 13.4 points where it is far below, and the strong-mismatch baseline is a model emitting 22K-token unterminated responses, so much of what is demonstrated is repair of breakage rather than transfer of reasoning. Two objective-level findings recur. Token agreement is not the goal: a student can drive agreement to near-perfect by looping while the response is globally broken, so the metric is satisfiable by degeneration. And preferences established under gold labels understate themselves under noisy ones -- full-vocabulary divergence beats sampled-token distillation by 17.8 and 7.2 percent under pseudo-labels against 2.0 and 2.7 under gold, while truncating to the teacher's top-100 entries matches the full vocabulary and is therefore free. The corpus's standing caution is that a distilled result is a statement about one teacher: several sources note their rationales, filters and quality audits all come from proprietary models, so what rationale supervision buys is what that teacher's rationales buy.
sparse autoencoder15Learning an overcomplete dictionary on model activations under a sparsity penalty, so that a polysemantic activation vector is decomposed into features that admit individual interpretation. Across 15 sources it is the dominant tool for this and the one the archive holds the most evidence against as a source of stable facts. The core problem is non-identifiability, and it is empirical before it is theoretical: different training seeds and dictionary widths recover materially different features from the same activations, dictionaries absorb and split features, and residual-stream components remain uncaptured by any known variant -- so two studies using different dictionaries are not measuring the same object. The theoretical companion in the corpus argues the failure is structural rather than algorithmic, since a reconstruction-and-sparsity objective does not enforce the invariance identifiability would require. Quality is also lossy and depth-dependent: automated interpretability scores decline from about 0.30 at layer 0 to 0.15 by layer 5, against about 0.14 for an independent-components baseline and 0.06 to 0.08 for random directions, and substituting reconstructed activations back raises perplexity from 25 to 40. Two scope limits recur: it must be trained per layer per model, which makes it costly and model-specific rather than endogenous, and it operates at the token level, which mismatches the unit reasoning happens in. The archive's practical position is that a sparse-autoencoder feature is a hypothesis about a direction rather than a measurement of one, and that any claim built on a specific dictionary should state the dictionary and, ideally, be checked against a second.
LoRA14Training low-rank adapter matrices while the base weights stay frozen, used across 14 sources as the default cheap fine-tuning method and, in several, as the thing that bounds what a study establishes. Configurations in the corpus cluster tightly -- ranks 8 to 256, alpha typically twice the rank, dropout 0.05, one to two epochs -- and one source's own sweep peaks at rank 128 while it reports rank 256, attributed to the sweep's smaller training set. Two findings are worth carrying. It is not always worth its cost: in one forecasting system it and full fine-tuning cost 8.21 and 15.21 times the training time to move the headline error by at most 0.001, which is the clearest instance in the archive of a backbone that is close to inert for the task. And several sources name it as a scope limit on their own conclusions -- training on 1,000 problems for one epoch on a single GPU bounds what a comparison establishes about full fine-tuning at scale, and one contamination study notes its clean reference models are themselves adapters at one hyperparameter setting. It is also the mechanism by which several archived methods keep a frozen model intact while adding a capability alongside it, restructuring the residual stream through an adapter rather than editing the model.
PPO14Policy optimisation with a clipped surrogate objective, a learned value function and a KL penalty -- the generation before the critic-free group-relative methods that dominate this archive, and across 14 sources still the source of the machinery those methods keep. The corpus's most useful content concerns its critic. Scalar regression is statistically consistent for exactly the quantity needed, since with binary terminal rewards the return from an intermediate state is Bernoulli and its conditional mean is the probability of eventual success -- one source is explicit that scalar critics are not representationally invalid. The failure is calibration rather than representation: because the critic enters the actor update as a subtraction, a modest overestimate gives failed rollouts large negative advantages and successes small positive ones, measured at a 2.63 ratio and growing more skewed over training, so the update spends most of its magnitude on failures. A distributional critic brings that ratio to 1.02 and recovers pass@256 from 38.48 to 48.06 -- and the archive should note the baseline number, since 38.48 sits below the untrained base model's 40.74, so unmodified training narrowed the boundary. Two comparative findings: against group-relative optimisation under an identical reward it loses 72.6 to 81.2 where correctness is localised to a few tokens, attributed to batch-level rather than group-relative advantage normalisation, and token-level reward weighting that helps the group-relative method severely destabilises it. Its clipping and importance sampling are also the mechanism one source blames for post-training concealing benchmark contamination.
component ablation12Removing one part of a method at a time and reporting what each removal costs, and across 12 sources the archive's default evidence that a design is more than the sum of arbitrary choices. What the corpus establishes is mostly how to read one. Removals frequently improve some benchmark while degrading others -- one source reports every single component ablation gaining on at least one of three sets, with removing a prioritisation term gaining 0.4 on one and losing 1.8 and 2.0 on the others -- so the honest conclusion is that the full configuration is best on average rather than everywhere, which those authors state and others do not. Sequential ablation reports ordered marginals, not interaction-free contributions, since a different addition order gives different numbers; one source says so explicitly while presenting a monotone sequence. And a removal that hurts establishes that a component carries information, not that the method uses the property it was designed around -- for that the archive's standing requirement is a control that preserves the component and breaks only its alignment, or a random alternative of matched cost. The strongest instances in the corpus are those where the ablation isolates a mechanism rather than a number: restricting a method to the subpopulation it claims to act on and recovering 84.7 percent of the gain, or removing two control actions separately and finding both load-bearing and not interchangeable (15.31 and 13.06 average F1).
retrieval-augmented generation11Conditioning generation on retrieved documents, and in this archive mostly a setting other things are measured in rather than a method studied on its own -- it supplies the substrate for search-agent credit assignment, evidence-grounded guardrails and long-document question answering across 11 sources. The one explicit claim the corpus makes about it is a negative: grounding an answer in retrieved documents does not guarantee the answer follows from them, so a separate guardrail is needed to judge whether a claim is supported by the evidence it cites. That gap is what several archived systems are built to close, by verifying causally rather than by construction -- masking the evidence a system cites flips 82.8 percent of correct answers while masking uncited evidence changes 9.6, which is what a grounding claim needs to look like. Its other recurring role is as the setting where credit assignment is hardest, since a trajectory of many retrievals receives one scalar and nothing distinguishes the retrieval that found the key document from one that repeated a query.
ablation10Removing something -- a feature, a direction, a component, a view -- and reporting what breaks, used across 10 sources both as evidence and as the thing being argued against. The corpus's most useful content is about when it misleads. One source argues explicitly against it for locating a representation, on the grounds that when a representation is split or partially redundant across latents, removing one produces false negatives, and steers along the decoder direction in both signs instead. A second shows removal answering a different question from the one asked: removing a direction from the trunk, from the diffusion module, or from both fails to reduce the generated content, so the trunk signal is sufficient for decodability and not necessary for generation. A third bounds its own claim in the sentence that makes it, noting that a suppression experiment establishes a representational effect on reconstruction and not a causal effect on receiver behaviour, because downstream outputs were never measured after the intervention. And selectivity is fragile: in one cross-model study a language-selective ablation hurts the target language significantly more in all 17 models while selectivity for the other language is statistically indistinguishable from zero in 12 of 17, with the direction of the asymmetry reversing under a different attribution method. Where the corpus is positive it is about ablations that are matched and exhaustive rather than single: one reports every removal degrading both metrics in essentially every setting and reads the ordering, and several note that a complete or near-complete suppression without a matched null -- a random component of comparable activation volume -- shows that something changed rather than that the right thing did.
DAPO10A group-relative variant that removes the value network and adds clip-higher, dynamic sampling, token-level policy-gradient loss and overlong reward shaping, used across 10 sources as both a strong baseline and a source of components other methods adopt. Two of its choices have explanations in the corpus. Clip-higher works because relaxing the upper clip for positive-advantage samples preserves exactly the entropy-increasing updates -- positive-advantage updates raise entropy and negative-advantage ones lower it -- so the mechanism is entropy preservation rather than a generic loosening. Its overlong soft penalty is reused directly by later methods. As a baseline it is beaten in ways worth noting: a distributional critic raises pass@256 from its 42.34 to 48.06, and standard group-relative training given a bounded reward mapping beats it and three other optimisation-level variants each given the naive unbounded reward -- which one source reads as showing reward-level and optimisation-level fixes are not interchangeable. One caution about a result reported on its training distribution: in one study the headline gains come from a held-out split of the training set rather than an independent benchmark, where the same model's competition-benchmark improvements are close to flat.
process reward model10A learned scorer for intermediate reasoning steps, and across 10 sources the standard route to dense supervision -- with the standing objections that it needs step annotations, a separate training run, or online judging, all of which the archive's cheaper alternatives are designed around. Two findings worth carrying beyond the cost argument. Its ranking for candidate selection does not carry over to its use as a policy model: in one comparison only the weakest selection variant improved policy performance, a disconnect that source reports without explanation even though the two uses are what such a model is for. And the training cost differs by more than an order of magnitude between constructions -- one rollout-based method requires 38.6 to 38.8 times the FLOPs of a cross-entropy implicit variant, and 21.3 to 146.5 times a preference-based one depending on rollouts. It also appears as the verifier in compute-optimal test-time allocation, where the allocation rather than the scorer is what buys the fourfold efficiency.
circuit analysis9Identifying the subgraph of components a model uses for a task, and across 9 sources a method whose reach the corpus is explicit about. Its scope has been limited to partial evidence or to small models -- three-layer transformers and GPT-2-scale networks -- and the sources that extend it beyond that do so in controlled synthetic settings, which is what makes the analysis and the in-distribution/out-of-distribution split possible while leaving open whether the same structure arises during realistic pretraining. Two self-imposed caveats recur. The analysis often operates at the level of attention patterns, and the authors of one source say it would be better strengthened by embedding-level analysis. And an architectural or mechanistic suggestion that follows from a circuit is a hypothesis rather than a tested claim; several sources mark that boundary themselves. The archive's related material bears on it from the identifiability side: a decomposition that is not invariant to the procedure that produced it is not comparable across studies, which is a question circuit work inherits whenever its units come from a learned dictionary rather than from the weights.
Monte Carlo tree search9Building a search tree by simulation, using visit counts and value estimates to decide where to expand, applied across 9 sources to reasoning traces, report generation and physical test planning. Two structural properties transfer beyond any one application. Progressive widening decouples the search from the size of the action space: across grids spanning a 36-fold range in action cardinality the realised tree is identical at 500 nodes and 30 root children, because children expanded at a node are bounded by its visit count -- so refining the action resolution raises attainable quality at no combinatorial cost. And the budget curve carries a condition: yield rises smoothly and sublinearly with iterations on a feasible instance while a fourfold increase changes nothing on an infeasible one, so per-instance sensitivity rather than the aggregate scaling curve tells you which regime you are in. The archive's related material supplies the counterweight for language settings: a tree search over reasoning branches can lose badly to a single chain when branches interpret the problem inconsistently and the search has no scorer able to adjudicate between them.
logit lens8Decoding an intermediate activation through the output unembedding to see what token it would produce, used across 8 sources as a cheap read-out of what a layer is holding. The corpus is consistent about its status: it gives a relative ordering rather than a calibrated probability, since intermediate states are not calibrated for direct decoding, and one source keeps it in raw form deliberately for cross-model comparability. That caveat has a direct methodological consequence the archive should carry -- a region or state that fails to decode may be one whose content is not yet expressed in the unembedding basis rather than one that lacks the content, so a negative logit-lens result is weak evidence. Its productive uses here are comparative rather than absolute: agreement with the sampled token rising gradually across depth in one model and jumping over three layers in another; regions attended for real objects decoding to the target token while regions attended for hallucinated ones do not; and, at scales where no full-rank reference can be trained, a trained alternative catching attacks the raw lens misses (0.80 against 0.68).
on-policy self-distillation8Distilling a model into itself under a different context: the teacher branch is the same weights given a privileged input the student will not have at inference, and the student is supervised on its own rollout prefixes. Across 8 sources the framework is settled and the open questions are all about the coefficient and the context. On the coefficient: standard practice gives every local divergence the same weight regardless of position or of the divergence sequence it sits in, and the archived variants differ in what they weight by -- turn-level Bayesian belief updates, divergence-adaptive supervision horizons, reasoning-pivot tokens identified by the teacher's shift when a reference is added or removed, or hindsight-derived per-turn multipliers. One of them reports the useful null: unit-weighted dense supervision, which is what a method without allocation delivers, improves one metric by 6.4 points and yields no consistent gain on three others, so density alone does not explain these results. On the context: the corpus's options are a reference solution (specifies the target rather than the evidence), a skill card (no answer but no query-specific information), a completed trajectory with its verified outcome, or a retrieved evidence set -- and every source that tests it runs a scrambling control, because a privileged field must be matched rather than merely present. The standing limitation several sources list for themselves is that it requires a privileged teacher and so does not apply where none exists.
attention analysis7Reading attention weights as evidence about what a model is using, across 7 sources both a productive signal and a repeatedly qualified one. Its productive uses here are comparative rather than absolute: attention paid to the end-of-thinking token from that position ranks which reasoning steps matter, validated by ablation showing that pruning the highest-ranked steps spikes the answer's perplexity while pruning the lowest raises it marginally and random pruning falls between; and cross-phase routing focus separates reasoning hallucinations. Its recurring qualification is that attention says where a model looked, not what it found -- one hallucination study finds attention alone does not separate real from hallucinated objects and only what the attended region decodes to does, and another notes that a circuit analysis operating mostly at the level of attention patterns would be better strengthened by embedding-level analysis. The archive's standing note applies: high attention correlated with a downstream quantity is a correlation between one position and one measurement, not evidence that information is routed through that position causally.
clip-higher7Relaxing the upper clipping bound in a PPO-style surrogate while keeping the lower one, introduced with DAPO and adopted widely as an entropy remedy. Across 7 sources the corpus supplies both its mechanism and its limits. The mechanism is specific: updates on positive-advantage samples are entropy-increasing and updates on negative-advantage samples entropy-decreasing, so relaxing the upper clip preserves exactly the entropy-increasing updates -- which makes it an asymmetric intervention rather than a general loosening. Its limit is measured: it raises entropy and helps early but becomes unstable, saturating and then declining, where a covariance-based penalty gives stabler curves; and in one comparison its response length saturates around step 200 against 300 for an alternative. One source flags an internal tension worth noting -- clipped tokens are excluded from its analysis on the grounds that their gradients vanish, and the same framework is then used to explain clipping mechanisms, so the interpretation is about which tokens escape clipping rather than about clipped tokens themselves.
entropy regularization7Adding an entropy term to the objective to slow policy collapse, and across 7 sources the baseline that the covariance and clipping remedies are compared against. Its measured standing is middling and consistent: on one comparison it reaches 47.8 and 48.0 average against clipping variants at 48.9 to 50.7 and a later method at 51.9 to 52.4, and on another it escapes degeneracy late in training but buys its diversity mostly through incorrect rollouts -- which is the archive's recurring point that entropy is not exploration. The corpus's structural criticism is that it acts on a summary statistic rather than on the mechanism: the covariance account identifies which token updates raise or lower entropy, and an undirected bonus does not distinguish them. One source also notes that the closest prior theoretical account depends on the advantage of tokens never sampled, which most algorithms cannot estimate, so it is hard to act on.
matched-budget comparison7Comparing methods at equal compute rather than at equal steps or equal samples, and across 7 sources the control that most often changes a reported conclusion. Its instances in the archive are specific. Rollout-matched against equal-FLOP training: a baseline given 88.0 GPU-hours scores 45.52 and given 131.2 scores 46.31, so the extra updates buy 0.79 and the method's margin is 4.06 rather than 4.85. Matched generation-token accounting: pointwise allocators already beat full-group training substantially under a shared budget, so a method must be compared against them rather than against the full-group default. And matched inference: one contrast method reaches 35.19 in 6h14 at 8 rollouts while direct sampling reaches 34.42 in 22h41 at 64 -- worse than at 16 -- so the comparison against simply sampling more is the sharpest available test. The corpus also records where matching is declined and why: one source holds the student-update count fixed as the right control for its claim, reports teacher sampling cost, verifier calls, runtime and peak memory in a separate table rather than equalising them, and states plainly that nothing there shows its method wins at equal total budget. The archive's position is that an unmatched comparison is not worthless but is a different claim, and that saying which one is being made is the minimum.
on-policy distillation7Supervising a student on its own rollouts using a teacher's distribution at those prefixes, which removes the train-inference mismatch of learning from a teacher corpus and gives dense token-level signal where an outcome reward gives one bit. Across 7 sources its objective and its ceiling are both under question. The objective is token agreement, and that has a named failure: a student can drive agreement to near-perfect by looping while the response as a whole is broken, so the metric is satisfied by degeneration rather than by learning -- with the two directional causes separately identified as student-excess tokens whose log-ratio corrections grow unbounded and student-deficit tokens no on-policy update ever reaches. The ceiling is the sharper finding. Measured from K=1 to K=1024, it raises average correctness at every budget while its pass@K advantage reverses at large budgets, a problem-level accounting finds more problems forgotten than learned with the retained set carrying the gain, and a perplexity analysis shows mass moving toward paths the base already supported -- so what transfers is sampling efficiency rather than capability. Four published variants reproduce the pattern and off-policy distillation, evaluated identically, does not. One further caution about effect sizes: one improvement's margin is 1.0 point where the student is close to the teacher, inside the spread of three other baselines, and 13.4 where it is far below -- and the strong-mismatch baseline is a model emitting 22K-token unterminated responses, so much of what is demonstrated there is repair of breakage.
reasoning distillation7Training a smaller model on long reasoning traces from a larger one, and across 7 sources both a widely used capability transfer and the comparison that complicates the archive's reinforcement-learning results. Two findings. It does transfer capability where on-policy methods do not: evaluated identically across sampling budgets, off-policy distillation is higher on both coverage and average correctness at every budget while on-policy distillation's advantage reverses at large budgets. And it is the right control for several claims: one exploration study's controlled comparison is verifiable-reward training against long-chain distillation from the same base rather than against the base model, so 'reduces exploration' there means relative to a distilled policy trained on the same data. The corpus's caution is about attribution -- one source's strongest supervised arm learns from a 14B distilled reasoning model while its reinforcement arm has no teacher at all, so a headline supervised-versus-reinforcement comparison confounds the trace source with the training method. Several archived efficiency methods distil pruned or compressed traces rather than raw ones, and one head-to-head finds step-level importance criteria agreeing on what to keep while disagreeing on what to cut, because redundancy is diffuse rather than located in an identifiable class of step.
REINFORCE7The plain policy-gradient estimator and its baseline-corrected variants, present across 7 sources mainly as the reference point that locates the group-relative methods. Two results place it. Its induced prompt weighting is uniform over difficulty, which makes it the high-temperature limit of the softmax group-advantage family and the opposite end from maximum-likelihood weighting -- and in a controlled setting where exact maximum likelihood is available as cross-entropy, it makes no progress at all from low initial success rates even at 1024 rollouts, while group-relative training improves and softmax advantages track cross-entropy closely. And its variants are largely interchangeable on the measures that matter here: six algorithms including it show only minor variation in the sampling-efficiency gap and all remain far from the base-model bound, and one entropy analysis finds its fitted curves unchanged across four of them. The archive's reading is that the choice among these estimators is a smaller decision than the choice of what the reward and the grouping express.
Coconut6Feeding a model's last hidden state back as the next input embedding so that reasoning proceeds in continuous space rather than through emitted tokens, and across 6 sources the reference implementation of latent reasoning -- along with the archive's clearest evidence about what such methods do and do not achieve. Its measured trade is domain-dependent: on grade-school arithmetic explicit chain of thought reaches 42.9 percent at 25.0 tokens against its 34.1 at 8.2, while on a structured logic task it reaches 97.0 percent at 14.2 tokens against explicit chain of thought's 77.5 at 49.4 -- so it wins where search over a constrained space is the bottleneck and loses where the reasoning is arithmetic. Two negative results matter more. It is fragile to train: removing the curriculum collapses it to at or below the no-reasoning baseline on two of three tasks. And in the fine-tuned regime its latent tokens can be inert -- deleting every one of them changes accuracy by at most 1.0 point across four models, with entity probing showing the final answer dominating the belief distribution from step 0 with no progression, against a discrete baseline where the target takes over only at the last step.
curriculum learning6Ordering training material from easy to hard, or staging what is supervised, and across 6 sources a component whose removal is often the largest ablation in the paper. The sharpest instance is latent reasoning: removing the curriculum collapses one method to 14.4 percent on a grade-school set, 52.4 on one logic set and 76.1 on another -- at or below the no-chain-of-thought baseline in two of three cases -- so the method is not merely helped by staging but fragile without it. A staged curriculum graded by nesting depth is worth 8.78 points in one grounded chart-reasoning system, and removing a staged curriculum costs a further 0.51 to 1.05 mean F1 on top of the latent mechanism itself in another. The archive's related evidence on staging is consistent: initialising a step-level objective before a self-correction objective beats joint training and beats plain reinforcement learning by 2 to 4 points, so the ordering rather than the objectives carries part of the effect. The caution is the usual one for multi-stage pipelines -- attribution is hard, and one source's stage-wise ablation shows most of its headline compression arriving with the final stage rather than with the criteria the paper is about.
early exit6Stopping generation before the model would stop on its own, and across 6 sources a family whose reported savings are real and whose accuracy claims need care. The measured trade: one method averages 63.06 percent accuracy at 11.1 percent token reduction across three models and three benchmarks, against 59.78 and 58.70 for two competitors -- and the same source reports that early exit does not beat unrestricted generation on accuracy, with vanilla at 45.02, 74.32 and 73.69 against its 44.44, 73.3 and 71.45. So the honest framing is a savings method with a small accuracy cost rather than a free improvement. The comparison that establishes a stopping rule is doing work is against fixed truncation at matched savings: one probe recovers 89 percent of full-trace accuracy while saving 39 percent of tokens where fixed-percentage truncation at the same savings loses 23 accuracy points. The archive's related caution is that a stopping signal built on the model's own confidence inherits its calibration, and on hard problems uniformly high confidence indicates a failure to branch rather than a settled answer.
few-shot prompting6Supplying worked examples in the prompt, and across 6 sources a baseline whose standing depends on what kind of model is being prompted. Two sources report the same reversal from opposite directions. For reasoning-specialised models, zero-shot free-form generation now beats few-shot, and the paper reports its honest exception -- a general model without reasoning tuning collapses to 67.3 percent zero-shot free-form, below its few-shot results, with only zero-shot chain of thought beating them -- so the claim is about reasoning-specialised models rather than about few-shot prompting universally. And for a model already instruction-tuned in a target language, few-shot prompting at 48.80 percent slightly exceeds its own rationale fine-tuning at 45.36, mirroring the finding that the weakest general backbone gains most from added supervision. The archive's operative reading is that few-shot examples substitute for training the model does not have, and stop paying once it does.
KL regularization6Penalising divergence from a reference policy during reinforcement learning, present in nearly every archived training recipe at coefficients typically around 0.001. Across 6 sources it appears mostly as machinery rather than as a subject, and the informative uses are diagnostic. Its trajectory is read as evidence: one source reports KL from the frozen model rising steadily as an argument against representational collapse while noting this does not rule out subtler coupling, and another finds the strictest reward tolerance producing the lowest KL trajectory -- meaning the policy never left its initialisation, which is how a sparse reward fails silently. One source names it as part of the trust-region approximation that sits outside its own theorems, so results proven for an unclipped unregularised objective do not transfer to the deployed method. And it is the standard remedy proposed for general-capability forgetting during reasoning training, though the archive's evidence on that is that the damage is often localised to one language or one benchmark rather than diffuse, which a single divergence penalty is poorly shaped to address.
PCA6Projecting activations onto their directions of greatest variance, and across 6 sources the baseline that decomposition methods must beat and frequently do not by much. Two careful comparisons. On automated interpretability across residual-stream layers, sparse coding scores about 0.30 at the first layer declining to 0.15 by the fifth, against 0.07 to 0.13 for principal components and 0.06 to 0.08 for random directions -- so the ordering is right and the margin narrows with depth. On causal transport, ablating the top-j modes of an alternative decomposition moves a behavioural readout more than j random directions and less than j principal directions (at j=8, principal components remove 60 percent against the modes' 25), with the gap decaying 4.1-fold in depth-distance and essentially gone by about 8 -- read by those authors narrowly, as principal components ceasing to be the better transport basis without the alternative being shown to be one. The archive's use of this pair is as a template: report the simple baseline and the random null together, and let the margin between them bound the claim.
sparse dictionary learning6Finding a set of directions such that every activation vector is a sparse linear combination of them -- the formal statement of what a sparse autoencoder approximates, and the method the superposition hypothesis implies if features are assigned to an overcomplete set of non-orthogonal directions. Across 6 sources the archive's evidence is the same as for its dominant implementation: it beats the alternatives on automated interpretability (about 0.30 at the first layer against 0.14 for independent components and 0.06 to 0.08 for random directions) and declines with depth to about 0.15 by the fifth layer, its reconstruction is lossy enough to raise perplexity from 25 to 40 when substituted back, and its solutions are not identifiable, with different seeds and widths recovering materially different features from the same activations. One source in the corpus argues the non-identifiability is structural rather than algorithmic, since a reconstruction-and-sparsity objective does not enforce the invariance identifiability would require.
VeRL6The reinforcement learning library these sources build their training pipelines on. None describes its design; it appears as the implementation substrate, cited to establish that a comparison against a baseline is like-for-like — that a method differs from its GRPO baseline only in the token-selection step with loss and optimizer identical, or that it follows the DAPO recipe inside the same codebase. Its recurrence across five independent entries is itself a fact worth recording about the archive: much of the entropy literature here is measured on one framework, so an artefact of that framework would not show up as a disagreement between them.

AIME 202452Thirty competition mathematics problems from the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination, and by source count the archive's most used hard benchmark -- 52 papers. Its properties as an instrument are what the archive should carry. It is thirty problems, so a single problem is 3.3 percentage points and most reported differences on it are one or two problems; one source flags this itself, noting that pass@k comparisons at large k are more stable than accuracy deltas but the absolute count stays small. It is where methods separate most: entropy-control work reports its largest margins here (15.0 points against 14.6 on the 2025 edition and far less elsewhere), and parameter-space exploration reports up to 5.83 points here against fractions of a point on easier sets. It is also where averages are carried or destroyed -- one paper notes its own five-benchmark average is carried by the easier sets because this one sits at 5.21 and 7.71 at small scale, and another reports its objective buying tail coverage at visible cost in the columns for this benchmark. Two robustness findings attach to it. Under a symbolic variabilisation that replaces its constants with sampled values and requires consistency across instantiations, strict-metric accuracy falls 58.8 percent on average, though the loose metric that grants partial credit shows far milder drops (1.5 percent for one 32B model), which separates contamination from inconsistency. And it is the benchmark on which the pass@k-versus-CoT-pass@k disagreement about whether reinforcement learning expands the reasoning boundary is sharpest, because easier sets are already solved by the base model with enough attempts.
MATH50049A 500-problem subset of the MATH benchmark, and by source count the archive's default mid-difficulty mathematics evaluation -- 49 papers. Its role is structural: with 500 problems it is large enough that differences are not single-item noise, which is why several sources note their five-benchmark averages are carried by it and by GSM8K while the 30-problem competition sets swing. That makes it the right place to look when a headline average moves and the reader wants to know whether anything stable moved with it. Its position in difficulty produces two recurring patterns. Compression and efficiency methods report their largest savings here -- 53 percent token reduction on the top decile of problems and 81 percent on the top percentile -- because a mid-difficulty set contains many problems the model overthinks. And methods aimed at hard reasoning report muted effects, since the base model already solves it given enough attempts. Two cautions from the corpus. It has been used as the source material for adversarially rewritten evaluation sets, so a set derived from it has whatever difficulty the rewriting model produced. GSM8K428,500 grade-school arithmetic word problems, introduced in the archive's oldest source alongside the finding that training a verifier to rank sampled solutions beats finetuning the generator directly. Across 42 sources it is the easy rung of nearly every suite, and its role has changed: later archived work treats it as saturated, uses it to check that a method aimed at hard problems has not broken easy ones, and raises contamination concerns about publicly available mathematics benchmarks in general. Two things make it worth keeping in a suite. Its size means a five-benchmark average is often carried by it and by MATH500 while the 30-problem competition sets swing, so it is where a claimed average improvement is either substantiated or exposed. And per-task ablations frequently invert on it -- one method's largest loss is removing thought-matching here and removing the latent thought entirely on a harder set, and one steering study finds influence falling at the end of a trace here while rising at the end on two other benchmarks. AIME 202538Thirty problems from the 2025 American Invitational Mathematics Examination, used across 38 sources as the harder and less contaminated companion to the 2024 edition. Its release after many base models' training cutoffs is the property that makes it distinctive rather than redundant: one source attributes the sharpest gap in its pass@k-versus-CoT-pass@k comparison to exactly that freedom from contamination. It is where test-time-scaling methods report their largest margins -- one reaches +13.4 points on it against small gains on easier sets, and a sequential-scaling baseline plateaus around 22 percent at 8K thinking tokens and does not improve at 32K while the compared method climbs to 35.6. That same source states the arithmetic honestly: 30 problems means +13.4 is four problems even at three seeds. The archive should also carry that internal signals behave differently here than on its 2024 sibling: Spearman correlation between one entropy diagnostic and average accuracy is 0.417 on the 2024 set and 0.047 on this one, so a diagnostic validated on one is not validated on the other.
AMC2323Forty problems from the 2023 American Mathematics Competitions, used across 23 sources as the middle rung between grade-school and Olympiad-level mathematics. Its size is the first thing to know: at 40 problems one item is 2.5 points, and at least one source explicitly flags reduced statistical power on it while reporting its largest swings there. Its position in difficulty is what makes it useful and what limits it. Length-compression work reports the clearest waste on it -- a median 2.7K emitted tokens against a median 830 needed for the correct answer -- and one compressed model exceeds its uncompressed original outright (77.5 against 72.5), which the authors read as redundant steps having introduced noise. Recovery from injected doubt cues tracks difficulty and places it between the easier and harder sets consistently across two backbones. Conversely it is where the pass@k argument about reinforcement learning is muted, because the base model already solves it given enough attempts, and where several methods' margins are small because the benchmark is near saturation. The most informative result attached to it is a robustness one: under symbolic variabilisation, strict-metric accuracy falls 47.9 percent on average, with reinforcement-tuned 7B models losing 22.9 to 94.5 percent and frontier models only 1.2 to 6.6 -- but the loose metric granting partial credit narrows the robust models' drop to 2.6 to 7.2 percent while a base model still loses 38.5, which separates memorised patterns from mere inconsistency.
GPQA-Diamond18198 graduate-level multiple-choice science questions, used across 18 sources as the archive's standard non-mathematical hard benchmark. Three properties make it distinctive. It is where monitorability is lowest -- dataset difficulty is inversely related to it, and this set scores at the bottom of the range in one study -- and where faithfulness failures are starkest: under KV-cache compression one method slightly exceeds the uncompressed model's accuracy at 57.9 percent while 90.2 percent of its correct answers carry unsupported reasoning, against 42.3 percent uncompressed, which is the archive's cleanest instance of accuracy preserved while the reasoning underneath it is destroyed. It is small: 198 problems, so a per-problem analysis is feasible and a single item is half a point, and it is the set on which majority-vote self-consistency was shown pre-registered to lower per-problem accuracy on 56.6 and 65.7 percent of problems for two small models. And it is the benchmark most likely to move in the opposite direction from a method's target -- one rubric-trained medical system gains 17.41 points here as a generalisation check, while its two components split, one contributing 8.69 and the other 3.53. Its four-option format means chance is 25 percent, which several archived results sit close to.
MATH17A competition mathematics benchmark used across 17 sources, most often as a training corpus and as the full set from which the 500-problem evaluation subset is drawn. In the archive it appears mainly as one column among five or seven, and the useful observations about it are comparative. It is where per-benchmark orderings most often contradict the average: one method with the best overall score loses it narrowly (67.22 against 67.40) while winning three others, and another reports its baseline taking it at one scale (84.0 against 84.2) while the average goes the other way. Ablations are task-dependent on it in a way that is informative -- on one method the largest loss on a grade-school set is removing thought-matching while on this one it is removing the latent thought entirely, so what a component contributes depends on the difficulty of the set it is measured on. Several sources train on it and evaluate transfer elsewhere, which means their generalisation claims rest on a mathematics-only training distribution; a number of them say so explicitly as a limitation.
OlympiadBench17A competition mathematics benchmark at olympiad level, used across 17 sources as the hard end of a suite alongside the AIME sets. Its practical role is as the set where efficiency and steering methods show their clearest gains, because it contains long traces with room to compress: one length-compression method raises accuracy from 56.77 to 59.85 on it while cutting average trace length 42.7 percent across its suite, and moderate positive steering of a reasoning-effort direction improves it as part of an average that rises at every model size from 1.5B to 32B. It is also one of the benchmarks where base models overtake reinforcement-trained ones as the sampling budget grows into the tens or hundreds, which is the archive's standing caveat about large-k comparisons on hard sets. None of the sources describes its construction or size.
DAPO-Math-17K12A 17,000-problem mathematics corpus that has become the archive's default training set for verifiable-reward reinforcement learning -- 12 sources use it, several with the same framework and near-identical hyperparameters. Its value here is as a shared control: because so many methods train on it, differences between them are more attributable to the method than they would otherwise be, and several sources hold step counts and total sampled rollouts fixed across arms on it. Its use also varies in one way worth noting -- some sources filter it by pass rate before training (discarding items above 15/16 or below 1/16), which changes the difficulty distribution the reported numbers describe. It appears both as the sole training corpus and as the reinforcement-learning stage following a supervised warm start on other data. None of the sources describes its construction. The caution the archive should attach is that a training corpus this widely shared makes cross-paper comparison easier and makes any property of the corpus itself invisible, since almost nothing in the archive trains on an alternative and reports the difference.
LiveCodeBench12A continuously updated code-generation benchmark, used across 12 sources chiefly as the out-of-domain check for models trained on mathematics. Two results make it more informative than that role suggests. It carries the largest single coefficient in one internal-signal correlation table, at Spearman -0.894 against average accuracy -- a strong negative for a mathematics-trained model, which is the archive's sharpest illustration that a diagnostic validated in domain can point the wrong way out of it. And it is where the pass@k debate goes the other way: on code, pass@k itself improves after verifiable-reward training, with one trained model beating its distilled starting point across six versions of the benchmark, so the boundary extends there even from a distilled model. Its continuous updating is the property cited for reduced contamination risk relative to fixed sets.
Minerva11A mathematics benchmark of university-level problems including physics and free-form answers, used across 11 sources as the domain-mismatched member of an otherwise integer-answer suite. That mismatch is its main value here. One study finds a post-training method improving on every other benchmark and not on this one, and attributes it to the training data being restricted to integer-answer mathematics while this set is not -- an explanation the archive notes is offered post hoc rather than predicted. Another finds an internal diagnostic's rank correlation with accuracy at -0.148 here against +0.417 on a competition set, and reversing again against a different accuracy measure, so a signal validated elsewhere is not validated on it. It is also one of the benchmarks where base models catch up with and surpass reinforcement-trained ones as the sampling budget grows into the hundreds. Neither its size nor its construction is described in the sources.
MMLU10A broad multiple-choice knowledge benchmark spanning many academic subjects, used across 10 sources as the general-capability check that a reasoning-focused intervention has not damaged something else. Its high baseline is what makes it useful in that role and limits it in others: at accuracies near 88 percent, test-time-scaling methods report gains of one to three points and one steering study finds negative steering cutting reasoning tokens on it while maintaining accuracy, which the authors read as models misallocating reasoning strength on easy questions. Its STEM subset appears as a mid-difficulty column in latent-reasoning suites. One transfer result attaches to it: structural geometry learned on grade-school mathematics probes at or above 0.85 here, while a correctness predictor trained on the same data falls to 0.64 -- the same dissociation the archive records between structure and correctness elsewhere.
MMLU-Pro8A harder, ten-option revision of the broad knowledge benchmark, used across 8 sources where the original is too saturated to discriminate. Its distinctive role in this archive is as the setting for the sharpest negative result about reasoning summaries: on outcome-discordant items, linear readers of a model-written summary sit at within-item AUROC 0.503 to 0.545 -- essentially chance -- while readers of the full trace retain 0.544 to 0.590. The source is careful about what that rests on, noting the analysis runs on this benchmark alone, on an unusually difficult discordant subset whose accuracy is 23 to 30 points below the held-out pool with traces 2.5 to 3 times longer. It is also where one influence analysis finds the distinguishing behaviour at the end of a trace rising rather than falling, opposite to a grade-school set, and where one safety-alignment pipeline's utility cost lands hardest on small models.
GPQA7The graduate-level science question set from which the archive's more commonly used Diamond subset is drawn, appearing in 7 sources as a general or out-of-domain evaluation column. It is also where one correctness-detection method has its largest margin over the runner-up (0.830 against 0.759, p below 0.001) while margins on easier sets in the same table are within 0.006 -- so a headline advantage on this benchmark can carry a claim that the rest of the table does not support. One quantization study reports it identical at the densest and most compressed settings while dropping at an intermediate one, which the authors treat as descriptive noise.
HumanEval+7A function-level Python synthesis benchmark with expanded test coverage in its plus variant, used across 7 sources as one column in code or general-capability suites rather than as a subject. ARC-Challenge3A multiple-choice science question set with a deliberately hard subset, used across 3 sources as the non-mathematical column in latent-reasoning and abstention suites. Two archived observations. It is where per-benchmark orderings contradict an average: one method with the best overall score loses it by 1.5 points while winning three others. And it is one of the benchmarks in a quantization study whose movements are large and unsystematic in both directions -- 75.46 to 78.96 on the easy split while a code benchmark falls -- a pattern the authors describe as more consistent with single-run noise than with a compression effect.
HMMT 20253A competition mathematics set used across three sources as the harder rung of an evaluation suite, alongside the AIME sets and easier benchmarks. Its role in this archive is as a small, hard set where differences are large in points and weak in evidence: the privileged-distillation work reports it among three thirty-problem competition sets whose macro average carries a 5.617-point gain with a bootstrap interval spanning 2.592 to 8.333, and the unsupervised self-distillation work reports the full-vocabulary objective leading by 7.2 percent on it under pseudo-labels against 2.7 under gold supervision. The verifier-free selection work uses it as one of three benchmarks where confidence-maximisation selectors drop below the base pass rate, falling 76.5 to 68.0 on one model. None of the sources describes its construction; what the archive should carry is that a handful of problems separates methods here, so intervals matter more than point estimates.
AlpacaEval2An instruction-following benchmark scored by LLM judges, used in the archived sources in two unrelated ways. As a judge benchmark it is part of the preference-evaluation family whose scores are reported not to track safety, world knowledge or instruction following, with style bias offered as the explanation. Separately, its questions serve as the vanilla control in an overthink-detection experiment, paired against versions modified by an overthink attack, where a pre-generation length probe predicts substantially longer reasoning on the attacked versions.
ARC-AGI2A benchmark of abstract visual grid puzzles used here for two different purposes, neither of which is measuring reasoning cleanly. One source uses its tasks as part of a logic pool for studying which training items respond to continued RL. The other is the caution: separating perception from reasoning with a two-stage pipeline shows about 80% of vision-language model failures on ARC-style tasks are perception errors rather than reasoning errors, so a score on it confounds seeing the grid with reasoning about it.
ARC-Easy2The easier split of the AI2 Reasoning Challenge, appearing in this archive twice as a general-knowledge evaluation set. In the dense-lens work it is one of five corpora -- with the Pile, 2WikiMultiHopQA, SciQ and WikiText-2 -- over which trained lenses are fitted and evaluated, and the setting where a lens-based prompt-injection detector at 70B catches attacks an untrained logit lens misses (0.80 against 0.68). In the agent-channel compression study it is one of nine benchmarks, and the one where accuracy rises under compression, from 75.46 to 78.96 -- which that paper reads as evidence its per-task movements are noise rather than as an effect. Neither source describes the benchmark itself.
BigCodeBench2A code-generation benchmark used in both sources as an evaluation set rather than a subject. Its most informative appearance is in the probing-robustness sweep, where it is one of two benchmarks whose disagreement is the finding: on this one, averaging hidden states over the response beats reading the last token by more than twenty points, while on the other the preference reverses -- and the two cancel in a pooled analysis, leaving a decisive factor ranked at under 1 percent of variance. It is also where the fitting data dominates, carrying 75 percent of variance against 16 for construction and 9 for the model, and where a synthetic fitting source drops the signal to 22 percent against a 25 percent chance floor. It appears again in the dynamic early-exit work. Neither source describes its construction; in this archive it is chiefly one half of a pair whose disagreement establishes that probing recipes do not transfer.
FinQA2A financial question-answering dataset built from real financial reports, used in both sources as the human-annotated point of comparison that symbolic generation is offered as an alternative to. Both make the same argument against it without disputing its usefulness: because it is constructed from real documents by annotation or model-assisted extraction, it conflates source-level noise -- formatting irregularities, missing values, ambiguous phrasing -- with genuine reasoning failure, and because ground truth is written by hand its cost makes broad coverage of reasoning depths prohibitive. Both respond by generating items from executable symbolic structures instead, so answers are correct by construction, difficulty is a controllable dial, and the benchmark can be regenerated free of contamination. Its one appearance as a measurement here is as a transfer check rather than a target: a model fine-tuned on verified generated traces answers 32 of 100 FinQA questions against a baseline of 27, reported explicitly as a proof of concept to test whether the gain was overfitting rather than as a result.
MathVision2A visual mathematics benchmark, used in two sources as the multimodal counterpart to the text-only mathematics sets. One audits a perturbation-based selection rule for vision-language test-time scaling on it and finds the rule's reported 31.8-point gain over majority voting is a decoding-format effect rather than a perturbation effect, once a control spends the same short-answer budget on the unperturbed image. The other includes it among the visual tasks where RLVR-trained models are measured against their base models with pass@k. Neither characterizes the benchmark itself, so what the archive records is the two experiments it appears in rather than what it contains.
MedCalc-Bench2A clinical calculation benchmark spanning equation-based subtasks -- laboratory values, physical measures, dates, dosage -- and rule-based ones covering risk, severity and diagnosis, notable in this archive because it is the only dataset carrying both calculation traces and clinical safety intervals. That second property is what one source builds on, using the safety interval as a hard reward constraint that a continuous precision term then refines within, and that source states plainly it evaluated on this benchmark alone because nothing else supplies both. The other source uses it among the settings where preserving final-answer accuracy under KV cache compression is shown to coexist with destroying the reasoning that supports it. Neither describes its construction; the archive's interest in it is that safety intervals make an asymmetric error cost explicit in the data rather than leaving it to the metric.
MT-Bench2A pairwise judge-evaluation benchmark of 2,391 comparisons with expert human judgments, and the most widely used basis for validating LLM judges. Two archived sources make it the cautionary case. Its balanced A/B/Tie label structure raises chance agreement, producing the largest kappa deflation of any benchmark measured — a cohort mean of 38.6 percentage points, so '85% agreement' corresponds to kappa around 0.48. It also compresses 21 judges into a 13.5-point kappa band where JudgeBench spreads the same models over 60.4, so small differences produce large rank changes. And judge preference measured on it is reported not to correlate with concrete measures of safety, world knowledge or instruction following.
ProofWriter2A rule-based deductive logic set, used in this archive by two papers with very different purposes and one shared caution about how its numbers are produced. The 1.58-bit quantization paper uses it as the scientific-logic arm of its evaluation and turns it into evidence about calibration rather than about logic: adding 256 ProofWriter training examples to the calibration mix moves the average from 39.50 to 82.21, against 84.92 for the full-precision model, so what the quantized model can still do depends on what it was calibrated on. ReLIT reports 98.60 on it as a theorem-finding set, far above the 82.40 of the best reasoning LLM in the same table -- but the archive's reading of that paper records that ReLIT was trained on 5,000 ProofWriter examples while every LLM number in the table is few-shot from GLoRE, so the comparison is not like for like. ReLIT's adaptive halting also uses the fewest recursion steps on it, 3.6 against 5.2 on RuleTaker and 7.8 on NaN-NLI, which that paper reads as the task being the least demanding of the four.
