Between Underthinking and Overthinking: An Empirical Study of Reasoning Length and correctness in LLMs

Jinyan Su, Jennifer Healey, Preslav Nakov et al. · preprint · 2025

An empirical study showing reasoning LLMs overthink easy questions and underthink hard ones, and that preferring shorter outputs via SimPO can cut generation length 30-60% with little accuracy loss.

  • Systematic sample-level and question-level empirical analysis showing a non-monotonic relationship between reasoning length and accuracy
  • Evidence that models lengthen responses appropriately for perceived difficulty on easy questions but fail to do so consistently on hard questions (underthinking)
  • Demonstration that excessive reasoning length degrades accuracy on a measurable fraction of questions (overthinking)
  • Application of SimPO with only correctness-agnostic length preference to reduce generation length 30-60% while largely preserving accuracy, using only unlabeled data

Results. Sample-level: accuracy vs. length-rank is non-monotonic — for R1-Distill, peak accuracy is at rank r*=1 on MATH and r*=3 on GSM8K; for R1-Preview, r*=1 on GSM8K but r*=0 on MATH (shortest response already best). For over 60% of questions across all model-dataset combinations, the shortest sampled response is already correct. Question-level: incorrect responses are significantly longer than correct ones across all four model-dataset pairs (Table 1); on MATH, incorrect responses average over 6,000 tokens vs. under 3,000 for correct ones (R1-Preview: 2459.84 correct vs 6106.17 incorrect; R1-Distill: 2907.23 correct vs 6521.31 incorrect). Pearson correlations between length and accuracy are consistently negative (e.g. -0.7248 for R1-Distill on MATH). On Easy-question advantage sets, models generate longer responses than on shared-easy sets (e.g. R1-Preview: 1015.37 to 1348.60 tokens on GSM8K, 1690.62 to 2832.86 on MATH), indicating models do lengthen for perceived difficulty on easy questions, but on Hard-question sets this pattern breaks down or reverses (Table 3), indicating models fail to recognize increased difficulty when questions exceed their capability. Rank-extremes analysis (Figure 7/8) shows that near the longest few samples, about 5% (R1-Preview) to 10% (R1-Distill) of questions that were correct at the second-to-last rank become incorrect at the very longest rank, evidencing degradation from excessive length. SimPO experiments: preferring shorter responses (regardless of correctness) reduces average token length by roughly 30%-60% while accuracy stays within an acceptable range without significant degradation across all four model-dataset settings; the length reduction is driven mainly by shortening incorrect (much longer) responses, but a 10%-25% reduction in length of correct responses is also observed, showing the model can somewhat adapt length to question difficulty even without correctness supervision — though overthinking is not eliminated.

Why it matters. The paper directly studies the accuracy/efficiency tradeoff of reasoning length in reasoning LLMs, empirically characterizing both overthinking (excessive reasoning degrading accuracy on easy/already-solved questions) and underthinking (failure to extend reasoning on hard questions), and tests a length-preference-optimization method (SimPO) as a mitigation — core to this topic's focus on when models think more or less than a problem needs.

ARM: Adaptive Reasoning Model

NeurIPS 2025 · 2025

ARM trains a model to pick among four reasoning formats (Direct Answer, Short CoT, Code, Long CoT) per task using Ada-GRPO, cutting average tokens by about 30% at roughly unchanged accuracy.

  • ARM, a reasoning model that selects among Direct Answer, Short CoT, Code and Long CoT per task without a human-specified token budget
  • Ada-GRPO, a GRPO variant with a frequency-based format diversity reward scaling and cosine decay that prevents collapse onto Long CoT
  • Identification and naming of format collapse as the failure mode of applying vanilla GRPO to multi-format reasoning training
  • Two additional inference modes: Instruction-Guided (user forces a format) and Consensus-Guided (three efficient formats vote, Long CoT on disagreement)

Results. Arm-7B vs. a Qwen2.5-7B SFT+GRPO Long-CoT baseline (maj@8): CommonsenseQA 85.7% vs 83.7% at -73.0% tokens; GSM8K 93.7% vs 94.8% at -60.1% tokens; MATH 82.6% vs 84.9% at -36.9% tokens; AIME'25 20.0% vs 20.0% at +7.9% tokens. Average 75.9% vs 76.1% accuracy at -32.5% tokens. The ~70% saving is on the easy commonsense end; on the hardest benchmark (AIME'25) ARM spends 7.9% MORE tokens than the baseline for identical accuracy, so the headline 30% average reduction is carried by easy tasks and does not hold where reasoning length actually matters. Ada-GRPO also gives ~2x training speedup, which follows mechanically from shorter rollouts rather than from any optimization change. Evaluated across Qwen2.5 3B/7B/14B in base, instruct and R1-Distill variants, on CommonsenseQA, OpenBookQA, SVAMP, GSM8K, MATH, AIME'25, Big-Bench-Hard, GPQA and StrategyQA.

Why it matters. Directly on topic, and it attacks the stopping problem from an unusual angle: instead of shortening a Long CoT, it lets the model decline to enter one. Ada-GRPO is a concrete answer to why the obvious approach fails — under plain outcome-reward GRPO the policy collapses onto Long CoT because that format wins on accuracy in every group, so any multi-format scheme needs an explicit force keeping cheap formats alive. The per-benchmark numbers are the more useful contribution for this topic than the headline: the 73% saving on CommonsenseQA and the +7.9% on AIME'25 together say the model is learning task difficulty roughly correctly, and that an average token-reduction figure across a mixed benchmark suite mostly measures how many easy problems the suite contains. Useful as a comparison point against methods that impose a length penalty on a single format.

Translation with Thought: Difficulty-Adaptive Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Domain Machine Translation

Yongshi Ye, Biao Fu, Chongxuan Huang et al. · 2026

TwT trains a translation model to spend reasoning tokens in proportion to input difficulty, by cold-starting on 7K difficulty-rewritten CoT traces and then running GRPO with a BLEU+COMET quality reward and an n-gram repetition penalty.

  • Documents reasoning collapse under pure R1-Zero-style RL for translation: traces fall below 100 tokens with or without KL, and three templates account for ~73% of Zh->En traces, so the emergent-reasoning result from math and code does not transfer to MDMT.
  • Quantifies the opposite failure: prompted general CoT uses 23.9x the tokens of parallel SFT on easy in-domain inputs and still scores 3.31 quality points lower; domain-aware prompting restores quality but raises usage to 42.9x.
  • A difficulty-adaptive cold-start dataset (~7K examples) in which an LLM judge rates input difficulty on four Rich-Point dimensions and rewrites each distilled trace to the matching depth.
  • A hybrid RL reward pairing BLEU with COMET after observing that a purely semantic reward is hacked — semantic score rises while BLEU degrades — plus a 20-gram repetition penalty on the reasoning trace.

Results. Quality is the mean of BLEU, COMET and CometKiwi; Tokens is the mean generated CoT length. In-domain (8 domains, averaged over En->Zh, Zh->En, De->En): TwT-14B 66.62 quality at 291 tokens and TwT-7B 66.27 at 274, against DeepSeek-R1 65.40 at 586, GPT-5 66.05 at 660, o3-mini 64.75 at 420, o1 65.58 at 433, QwQ-32B 63.55 at 693, and non-reasoning DeepSeek-V3 66.23 / Gemini-2.0-Flash 66.24 / GPT-4o 65.54. OOD (5 domains): TwT-7B 66.25 at 269 tokens vs DeepSeek-R1 65.62 at 561 (292 fewer tokens), Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking 66.71 at 1332, GPT-4o 67.25; TwT-14B 66.46 at 288. Against the same-paradigm RL baseline mExTrans-7B, TwT-7B is +4.45 quality with 50.27% fewer reasoning tokens. Multilingual: on 59 unseen FLORES+ languages, TwT-Gemma-2-9B-IT trained on 27K examples reaches 54.65 En->X and 67.04 X->En vs Tower-Plus-9B (same backbone, 286K examples) at 46.46/63.02, +8.19 on En->X; TwT-7B's En->X score of 41.13 is weaker than Gemma-2-9B-IT's own 54.28, so the multilingual result is carried by the Gemma backbone. Difficulty breakdown (Table 8, in-domain): TwT-7B Easy 67.93 at 216 tokens, Medium 64.85 at 272, Hard 62.78 at 332 — the trace does lengthen with difficulty; against General-CoT (Easy 61.54 at 311, Hard 56.53 at 551) that is 33% fewer tokens with +6.4 quality on Easy and +6.7 on Hard. Overall TwT is 32% below o3-mini and 60% below Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking in tokens, which is where the abstract's '32-60%' comes from — it is a range across two named comparison models, not a range of measured savings. Ablation (Table 6, dataset-level averages on a different scale from Table 3): removing the difficulty-adaptive rewriting leaves quality essentially unchanged (64.68 vs 64.77 in-domain, 66.17 vs 66.28 OOD) while length rises from 278 to 748 tokens; removing cold start collapses length to 62 tokens and drops quality to 64.18; SFT-only with adaptive CoT reaches 62.47 vs 61.65 domain-aware and 61.39 general CoT; the repetition penalty is a small regularizer (64.52 -> 64.77). Backbone transfer: Gemma-2-9B-IT 59.93 -> 64.71 in-domain, Llama-3.1-8B 59.84 -> 63.20. Redundancy audit by DeepSeek-V3.2 over 15 domains reports TwT resolving 87.3-97.2% of six redundancy types found in the SFT+RL baseline. Trace diversity: mean pairwise Sentence-BERT cosine similarity 0.89 for pure RL vs 0.51 for TwT. All numbers are software evaluations on A100 hardware; a small human/expert qualitative study covers 10 Zh->En examples.

Why it matters. On topic, and unusual for this archive in that it tests the length/accuracy tradeoff outside mathematics — the domain nearly every overthinking result we hold is measured in. Three things it adds. First, a clean negative result on the assumption that RL alone grows reasoning where reasoning helps: on translation, R1-Zero-style RL collapses traces to under 100 tokens of templated text regardless of KL, so 'let RL find the right length' is not domain-general. Second, a measured allocation curve rather than a single average — TwT's traces run 216 / 272 / 332 tokens on Easy / Medium / Hard inputs, which is the shape a difficulty-adaptive method should produce, and it holds quality above every LRM baseline at roughly a third to a fifth of their token counts. Third, a caution about where the adaptation actually comes from: the ablation shows dropping the difficulty-adaptive rewriting leaves quality within 0.1 points while length nearly triples (278 -> 748), and there is no length reward in the RL objective at all, so the length control is entirely inherited from what the cold-start traces look like. That makes this a data-shaping result, not a learned stopping criterion, and it is worth contrasting with the probe-based exit methods we track, which decide at inference time. The honest counterweight is that on this task reasoning buys very little to begin with: a plain parallel-SFT model with no chain of thought scores 65.52 in-domain against TwT-7B's 66.27, so the whole tradeoff is being measured over a range of under one quality point.

How Far Are We from Optimal Reasoning Efficiency?

NeurIPS 2025 · 2025

Defines an empirical accuracy-vs-token-budget frontier for a fixed base reasoning model, measures how far existing efficiency methods fall short of it with a single metric (REG), and proposes REO-RL, an RL objective that targets a handful of token budgets to close most of that gap.

  • The reasoning efficiency frontier: an empirical accuracy-per-token-budget upper envelope over many fine-tunes of a fixed base LRM
  • Reasoning Efficiency Gap (REG), one metric summing the shortfall from that frontier across all budgets, making accuracy-vs-length methods comparable
  • A systematic finding that existing efficiency methods fail in one of two distinct ways: brevity bought with accuracy, or high accuracy bought with far more tokens than the frontier requires
  • REO-RL, an RL algorithm approximating the full efficiency objective by numerical integration over a sparse set of token budgets (N=5 gives <1% approximation error)

Results. Base models DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B/7B and Qwen3-4B/8B, trained on 135k problems from DeepScaleR and AReaL, max generation 32K tokens, evaluated on AMC23, AIME24, AIME25 and Minerva Math. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B: vanilla RL reaches 64.4% accuracy at 11,282.6 average tokens; REO-RL (Exp) reaches 62.9% (-1.5 points) at 5,966.0 tokens, a 55.9% REG reduction; RL with a fixed 4K token budget reaches 54.1% at 2,677.0 tokens for only 27.5% REG reduction. Abstract-level headline is 74.5% and 64.2% REG reduction over vanilla outcome-reward RL at 1.5B and 7B. REO-RL gives >=50% REG reduction across all four models. Baselines compared: vanilla RL, RL with length-based rewards, hybrid reasoning (HGPO), SFT, and preference learning (SimPO). Note the frontier is constructed from the authors' own pool of fine-tunes, so REG is a relative measure and a method outside that pool could in principle sit above the frontier.

Why it matters. This is the measurement paper the topic needs. Every method in this area reports 'X% fewer tokens at comparable accuracy', and those numbers are not comparable across papers because each picks its own operating point on its own base model; REG replaces the pair with one number defined against a common frontier. Two findings matter for how the group reads other work here. First, the failure taxonomy — methods either buy brevity with accuracy or hit good accuracy at budgets far above what the frontier shows is attainable — means a paper reporting high accuracy is not thereby efficient, and vice versa. Second, targeting only five token budgets approximates the whole accuracy-per-budget curve to under 1% error, which says the tradeoff curve is smooth enough that dense supervision at every length is unnecessary. Caveat when citing: the frontier is the envelope of the authors' own fine-tune pool, so REG is a relative yardstick, and the evaluation is math-only.

Training-Free Inference-Time Self-Reflection and Cost-Bounded Early Stopping for Large Language Models

Wei Yu, Suxing Liu, Minjie Yu et al. · 2026

A training-free generate-critique-revise loop over a frozen backbone that stops when the critique emits a CONFIRMED sentinel or a depth cap is hit, measured across nine experiments to show the sentinel halts 82-88% of items at about 2.1 generations, with accuracy flat on BBH and significantly higher on GSM8K and MATH.

  • A training-free bounded generate-critique-revise protocol whose cost knobs are an explicit maximum depth and a CONFIRMED sentinel, requiring no gradient updates or trained verifier.
  • The measured finding that on clean multi-step reasoning the loop buys no accuracy (flat 74.0/74.0/74.0/73.0 across depths 1-4) but halts 82-88% of items at about 2.1 generations, reframing reflection's value there as bounded self-verification rather than improvement.
  • A confusion matrix quantifying the sentinel as a calibrated rather than random stop: 68% true positive, 16% false positive, 6% false negative, 10% true negative at the first reflection step on 500 BBH items.
  • A negative result reported as such: the model's own numeric confidence tag is uniformly overconfident, every threshold at or above 0.6 collapsing the loop to a single generation, so hypothesis H6 is marked REFUTED and the sentinel is identified as the mechanism that actually bounds cost.

Results. The paper's own headline is that the loop's value on clean tasks is bounded self-verification, not accuracy. On BBH the depth sweep is flat -- 74.0%, 74.0%, 74.0%, 73.0% for D = 1, 2, 3, 4, all inside a shared interval -- while the CONFIRMED fast path early-stops 82-88% of items and holds the average at about 2.1 generations. Component framing (E1: 73.3-74.0% across seven conditions at three temperatures) and component ablation (E2: removal changes accuracy by at most 4 pp) are both null. The meta-reward critique beats a plain 'check correctness' critique by +4.0 pp (73.0% vs 69.0% at comparable steps) but the paper reports this as directional, not significant, at n=100. A weak misleading-context probe costs 1.0 pp (73.0% -> 72.0%). The confusion matrix for the sentinel at the first reflection step (BBH, n=500) is the most informative single result: it emits CONFIRMED on 68% of items whose step-1 answer was already correct (true positive) and on 16% that were wrong (false positive, residual overconfidence that caps the loop's ceiling), while proposing a revision on 6% that were already correct (wasted compute) and 10% that were wrong (the correctable cases). So 78% of self-assessments are right, which is the paper's explanation for why aggressive early stopping does not cost accuracy. The confidence-threshold sweep refutes its own hypothesis H6: with the confidence instruction active the backbone declares first-generation confidence at or above 0.6 on 100% of questions, so every tau in {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} collapses the loop to a single generation, there is no selective working point, and the sentinel rather than the numeric tag is the effective cost-bounder. Cross-benchmark at n=500 the loop does raise accuracy with disjoint Wilson intervals: GSM8K 93.6% -> 97.8% (+4.2 pp, a net 21 items, 468 -> 489) at 2.15 steps with 88% early-stopped, and MATH 26.2% -> 40.4% (+14.2 pp, a net 71 items, 131 -> 202) at 2.42 steps with 82% early-stopped, with McNemar paired tests at p < 0.01 on both, computed conservatively from the marginal counts. Qwen2.5-72B reproduces the split: BBH 78.2% -> 78.4% (a net one item, not significant) at 81% early-stop, MATH 31.6% -> 43.2% (+11.6 pp, net 58 items, McNemar p < 0.05) at 78% early-stop. Token accounting: on BBH the loop costs 1,061 tokens/question at 2.06 calls versus 892 single-shot, against a depth-3 worst case of 2,676, so the sentinel cuts the unbounded-loop ceiling by 60%; on GSM8K it spends 993 tokens (+84% over single-shot) for +4.2 pp and on MATH 2,271 tokens (+156%) for +14.2 pp, saving 39% and 15% against their respective no-sentinel ceilings. Against matched-compute baselines at n=500, EvoResearcher reaches 74.0% / 97.8% / 40.4% on BBH / GSM8K / MATH at 2.15 generations and about 1,050 tokens, versus self-consistency k=3 at 74.5% / 96.8% / 35.6% for 2,670 tokens and fixed-depth Self-Refine at 71.2% / 97.0% / 36.8% for 2,650 tokens. Self-Refine's BBH score is the direct over-correction result: forcing three revision rounds on clean logical tasks costs 2.6 pp against single-shot's 73.8%, which the sentinel avoids. One number the paper flags against itself: the E6 session's single-shot anchor scored 81.0% on the same backbone and the same 100 BBH questions where the E1/E4 sessions scored 73.3-74.0%, about 7 pp of pure session variance, and within that same session the always-loop condition scored 76.0% against single-shot's 81.0%.

Why it matters. Squarely on topic: the paper is about when a model should stop revising, and it measures both halves of the tradeoff. Three results are worth carrying into the archive. First, the sharpest overthinking datum is in its baseline table rather than its own method -- fixed-depth Self-Refine, forced through three critique-revise rounds on clean BBH logic tasks, scores 71.2% against single-shot's 73.8% at three times the tokens. Reflection applied unconditionally makes correct answers worse, which is the phenomenon this topic exists to study, and it is measured here at n=500 against a matched single-shot control. Second, the paper supplies a mechanism for avoiding that, and quantifies how good the mechanism actually is: asking the frozen model whether its own answer needs revising produces a usable but imperfect signal, correct on 78% of items (68% correctly confirming a right answer, 10% correctly proposing a revision to a wrong one), overconfident on 16% and wasteful on 6%. That 16% false-positive rate is stated plainly as a hard ceiling on what any depth can achieve, which is a more honest framing of self-verification limits than most work in this area offers. Third, and reported as a refuted hypothesis rather than buried, the model's numeric self-reported confidence is useless as a stopping signal on this backbone: it declares confidence at or above 0.6 on 100% of first generations, so every threshold tested collapses the loop to a single shot and no selective working point exists. That is a direct negative result about a widely used class of confidence-based stopping rules, and it contrasts usefully with a discrete sentinel token, which on the same backbone discriminates. The paper's overall shape is the topic's central claim restated with evidence: reflection's value is task-dependent, worthless on tasks the model already solves (BBH flat across depths 1-4) and worth +14.2 pp where single-shot reasoning is unreliable (MATH, single-shot 26.2%), so the right policy is to spend compute conditionally rather than uniformly. What should be discounted: nearly all the clean-task experiments run at n=100 with an 8.7 pp confidence interval, and the paper itself documents about 7 pp of session-to-session variance on identical questions and backbone -- in the confidence-sweep session the loop scored 5 points below single-shot on BBH -- so the null results establish only the absence of a large effect, not calibrated equivalence. The n=500 GSM8K and MATH gains, with disjoint intervals and paired McNemar tests, are the parts that carry weight. The surrounding framework (evolving world, multi-agent swarm, GRPO objective) is unevaluated blueprint and irrelevant to the topic.

Towards Efficient Reasoning in LLM-Based Recommender Systems via Model Merging

Linh Dieu Le, Tong Chen, Shazia Sadiq et al. · 2026

REAM merges a slow-thinking recommender with a fast-thinking one at the granularity of individual attention heads, assigning each head a merge coefficient from its reasoning importance and its parameter sensitivity, to shorten reasoning traces without retraining.

  • Formulates reasoning compression in slow-thinking recommenders as a selective model-merging problem in which the fast-thinking update is allocated by each component's contribution to recommendation performance
  • REAM, a head-granular merging method that assigns every attention head its own merge coefficient from retrieval criticality, decision faithfulness and Fisher update sensitivity, solved in closed form as a water-filling solution to a constrained optimization
  • Two head-level reasoning-importance signals with causal validation by mean-ablation, shown to identify largely disjoint head sets (top-5% Jaccard <= 1.75%) that are stable across datasets
  • Experiments on Amazon Book, Yelp and Amazon Music showing 17.9-24.3% shorter traces with MAE and RMSE both better than the slow-thinking source, where eight training-free merging baselines fail to hold both

Results. Three rating-prediction datasets (Amazon Book, Yelp, Amazon Music) using Reason4Rec splits; metrics are MAE, RMSE and mean generated tokens per response (Tok), all lower-is-better, on a single A40 GPU. Against the slow-thinking source RecZero, REAM cuts tokens by 24.3% on Book (313.55 to 237.33), 17.9% on Yelp (314.54 to 258.19) and 21.1% on Music (344.28 to 271.49) while also improving MAE by 4.7%/2.6%/1.6% and RMSE by 2.3%/3.7%/2.2%. Among eight training-free merging baselines, REAM is the only one that improves both MAE and RMSE over theta_S on all three datasets: Task Arithmetic and DARE+TA compress harder on Book (253.13 and 252.81 tokens vs REAM's 237.33 — REAM is still shortest) but worsen MAE (0.6742 and 0.6835 vs 0.6650 for theta_S); AIM+TA and ACM+TA, which derive one coefficient per layer from activation statistics, worsen MAE to 0.6805 and 0.6932 on Book and produce longer traces (260.46, 282.74) than REAM. AIM+TA attains a better RMSE on Music (0.8227 vs REAM's 0.8324) but a worse MAE (0.5678 vs 0.5348); the authors read the recurring RMSE-improves/MAE-worsens pattern as a redistribution of errors rather than an accuracy gain. RAIN-Merging, the other head-granular baseline, underperforms REAM on every metric on all three datasets. Leave-one-out ablation (Music/Yelp): dropping Fisher sensitivity hurts most (MAE 0.5533 vs 0.5348 on Music; 0.7616 vs 0.7564 on Yelp); dropping kappa gives 0.5499/0.7599; dropping faithfulness gives 0.5350 on Music and slightly improves Yelp MAE to 0.7544 while worsening its RMSE and token count. Causal validation: mean-ablating the top-5% kappa heads raises NLL/JSD by 11.57x/3.29x on Music, 4.93x/2.86x on Book and 7.35x/3.19x on Yelp versus size-matched random-head ablation; faithfulness-critical heads give 9.65x/5.64x, 11.16x/5.20x and 6.63x/4.19x. The two signals' top-5% head sets are nearly disjoint (Jaccard <= 1.75%) and their Spearman correlation is negligible. Sparsity: the top 5% of heads hold 77-78% of retrieval mass (Gini 0.920-0.922) but only 22.6-24.0% of faithfulness mass (Gini 0.548-0.556). Cost: the full REAM pipeline takes 41.2 minutes / 0.69 GPU-hours per dataset, about 4% of the 15.7 GPU-hours needed to train the two source models. Transfer (Yelp only, Table 3): on Llama-3.2-3B REAM improves on theta_S across all three metrics (MAE 0.7769 vs 0.8059, Tok 257.18 vs 323.79) and on RecOne it improves MAE to 0.6876 from 0.7395; on Qwen2.5-7B it shortens traces from 336.02 to 288.46 and beats Task Arithmetic, but its MAE (0.7910) remains worse than the unmerged theta_S (0.7626).

Why it matters. On-topic. The paper is squarely about the accuracy/efficiency tradeoff of reasoning length, and its specific contribution to the topic is a weights-level control knob rather than a prompt, budget or reward: it shows that a slow-thinking model's verbosity can be reduced 17.9-24.3% by importing a fast-thinking sibling's parameters selectively, and that the selection matters — merging with one global or per-layer coefficient compresses comparably but costs accuracy, while protecting the ~5% of attention heads that carry 78% of evidence-retrieval mass keeps MAE and RMSE at or below the uncompressed source. That gives the group a mechanistic claim worth testing elsewhere: some heads sustain the reasoning that the answer actually depends on and others carry redundant elaboration, and the two are separable. The causal ablations (11.6x NLL increase from ablating retrieval-critical heads versus random) are the strongest part of that argument. Two scope limits matter for us. First, compression is baked into the merged weights, so it is uniform across inputs — this is not an adaptive stopping rule and does nothing about spending more on hard instances, which is the other half of the topic. Second, the setting is 1-5 rating prediction with 3B models and ~250-350-token traces, well below the long-CoT regime most overthinking work studies, and at 7B REAM still trails the unmerged slow-thinking model on MAE, so the approach is not yet shown to hold at scale.

Fewer Tokens, Smaller Cache: Reward-Coordinated Efficient Reasoning

Qiyuan Zhu, Dezhi Li, Pengyu Cheng et al. · 2026

ReCo uses a 30M process-reward estimator to set, per reasoning step, both the KV-cache retention ratio and generation-side controls (a reflection-token logit penalty and confidence-based early stopping), cutting generated tokens by 37-65% and end-to-end latency by 2.08-2.35x versus full-cache CoT.

  • Argues that on reasoning models KV-cache compression cannot be judged by eviction alone, since compressing the cache lengthens the reasoning it was meant to make cheaper, and quantifies that inflation (up to +79.0% average tokens; 79.8% of MATH-500 problems longer at a 25% rate).
  • Shows that a process reward tracks per-step compression tolerance: deleting the same token budget from high-reward steps beats deleting it from randomly chosen steps by 4.0/3.5 points (GSM8K/MATH-500, Llama-8B) and 6.5/5.9 points (Qwen-7B).
  • Proposes ReCo, coupling reward-adaptive KV retention, a reward-banded reflection-token logit penalty and confidence-probe early stopping under a single per-step reward, with no training of the reasoning model.
  • Reports 37%-65% fewer generated tokens, 2.08x-2.35x latency speedup and 37.7% lower peak memory over Full CoT across three models and six benchmarks, at the smallest accuracy drop among compared compressed methods.

Results. Averaged over three runs on a single NVIDIA H20 GPU. Accuracy (six-benchmark average) vs Full CoT: 60.2% vs 62.8% on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, 60.0% vs 61.9% on Qwen-7B, 69.6% vs 72.3% on Qwen3-8B - the smallest drop among compressed methods, where SnapKV falls to 37.5% and R-KV to 48.1% on Llama-8B. Average tokens fall 37% / 65% / 46% below Full CoT on the three models (Llama-8B 7078 -> 4491; Qwen-7B 5920 -> 2067; Qwen3-8B 6788 -> 3652), while cache-only baselines increase token count (SnapKV 7078 -> 11266 on Llama-8B). Latency speedup 2.08x / 2.35x / 2.18x, against 1.09-1.33x for cache-only baselines. Peak GPU memory on AIME25 (Llama-8B) 17.92 GB vs 28.78 GB full cache, 20.70 GB R-KV, 26.90 GB SAT. The accuracy advantage is largest on the hardest sets: AIME25 33.3% on Llama-8B where every KV baseline is <= 20%. It is not uniform: on Qwen-7B AIME24 SAT reaches 53.3% against ReCo's 40.0% and Full CoT's 43.3%, and on Llama-8B MATH-500 ReCo is 80.6% against Full CoT's 83.8%. Ablation (Llama-8B): each component alone is worse - KV compression alone spends 12.8k tokens on AIME25 against ReCo's 8.6k; reflection control and early stopping alone cut tokens but lose accuracy on hard sets. Setting delta = 0 (uniform, reward-agnostic retention) is among the weakest configurations (65.0% AMC). ReCo's own overhead is 0.60% (Pilot scoring), 0.11% (compression), 1.24% (reflection penalty) and 1.98% (early stopping) of end-to-end time. One configuration (lambda = 0.25, delta = 0.10, tau_p = 1.10) is used for all models and datasets.

Why it matters. Directly on topic, and it adds a mechanism the topic's usual framing misses: memory-side compression and reasoning length are coupled, not separable. The measured length inflation - R-KV at a 25% cache rate raising MATH-500 output from 3268.7 to 4538.1 tokens on Qwen-7B and 4409.8 to 7891.7 on Llama-8B, with 79.8% of problems longer - means an efficiency intervention can cause overthinking as a side effect, so any accounting of reasoning cost that stops at the cache is incomplete. It also supplies a concrete stop-at-the-right-point rule that separates two signals the archive should not conflate: process reward certifies the trajectory is sound, answer perplexity certifies the model is confident in the answer, and the paper stops only when both hold, arguing perplexity alone would license confident-but-wrong halts. The ablation that sets delta = 0 (uniform retention) and finds it among the weakest settings is evidence that per-step allocation, not just a smaller average budget, is what buys the accuracy.

ARES: Adaptive Reasoning-Effort Steering for PPA- and Cost-Aware RTL Optimization with LLM Agents

Stef Cuyckens, Mihaela Jivanescu, Jun Yin et al. · 2026

Ares is an LLM-agent RTL optimizer that raises the per-call reasoning effort only after progress stalls, and reports the normalized dollar cost of every call next to the power-area-delay figure of merit.

  • A normalized per-call dollar cost metric, reported alongside the figure of merit, that makes optimization quality and spend comparable across reasoning-effort levels and across optimizers
  • A cost-controlled comparison showing that an engineered cross-design long-term memory brings no dependable gain over a plain concatenation of the same experience, though both beat having no long-term memory
  • An adaptive per-call reasoning-effort policy driven by a patience counter fit once on training designs, escalating from medium to high only after progress stalls
  • Evidence on three held-out designs that the adaptive policy reaches a lower FoM than any fixed effort level at equal normalized cost, and cuts run-to-run variance by 58% on a multiply-accumulate case study

Results. All numbers are from a commercial synthesis flow (Synopsys DC + PrimeTime, Nangate 45nm), not from silicon; the agent runs on Claude Code with Opus 4.6, repeated multiple times because the LLM cannot be seeded. On the three held-out test designs (tv80 ALU, uart, AES controller) the adaptive policy ends at mean FoM 0.76, 0.77 and 0.73 against fixed high's 0.79, 0.84 and 0.79, i.e. it lowers FoM by 23-27% where the best fixed effort reaches 16-23%, at equal normalized cost. The fixed-effort sweep shows no level fits a whole run: low effort ends above medium everywhere and stops early because the LLM reports it is out of ideas; high effort lands its first successful optimization only after 4.6-7.1 high-calls where medium cuts its first 5% of FoM within 0.9-3.9; fixed high ends clearly deeper than medium only on tv80 (0.79 vs 0.84), the gap being 0.004 on uart and 0.016 on controller. Memory construction: with the same raw experience, the engineered memory and the plain concatenation descend together and end close on all three test designs with neither consistently ahead, though both end below the memoryless agent. On an LLM-drafted MX multiply-accumulate unit, six runs lower unnormalized FoM from 68.8 to a mean of 33.6 (deepest 27.5) against the hand-optimized MX_fp32's published 18.9, closing up to 83% of the gap; the same optimizer improves the already hand-optimized MX_fp32 by only 16%. Branching each of those six runs at its stall point, the escalated continuation beats the fixed-medium one in five of six (the sixth within 2%), lowering the mean from 42.1 to 33.6 and cutting run-to-run std from 8.9 to 5.8 (a 58% variance reduction) for 7.3 extra high-calls. Against prior optimizers on controller from the same v0 with 25 candidates each, Ares reaches FoM 0.694 at about 15 high-calls, REvolution 0.943 at about 10, and Dr. RTL 0.923 at 8.7x Ares's cost; Dr. RTL's timing-focused selection discards its own best power-aware candidate at 0.909.

Why it matters. A domain application rather than a study of reasoning length itself, but a substantive one: it treats per-call reasoning effort as a control variable and shows an escalate-on-stall policy beating every fixed level at equal spend (FoM lowered 23-27% vs 16-23%), which is the overthinking thesis measured in dollars instead of tokens. Two things here are worth carrying over. First, the unit of allocation is an iteration inside a multi-step agent run, not an isolated prompt - the authors note that prior budget-aware work (FrugalGPT, budget-aware tree search, compute-optimal scaling) adapts a single task, whereas here the difficulty of the next improvement changes as the design gets more optimized, so the difficulty signal has to be read from the run's own history. Second, the stall signal is entirely external: the counter charges on verified-but-no-gain and on failed candidates, never on anything inside the reasoning trace, which is a cheap difficulty proxy the archive's trace-inspecting methods do not use. The evidence that a fixed high effort is not merely wasteful but actively worse early in a run (first gain only after 4.6-7.1 high-calls, where medium gains within 0.9-3.9) is a concrete instance of overthinking costing time-to-first-improvement. Caveats for the topic: the effort knob is the provider's three-level setting, no reasoning-token counts or trace lengths are reported, and the accuracy axis is a hardware figure of merit rather than task correctness, so the tradeoff curve is not directly comparable to reasoning-benchmark results.
