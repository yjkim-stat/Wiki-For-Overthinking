# majority voting

<!-- auto:begin -->

Returning the most frequent answer among sampled trajectories, counting every trajectory equally. The sources treat it as the aggregation floor and report it is hard to beat outright but easy to improve on when combined rather than replaced — two reward-model variants that individually lose to it both surpass it once their scores weight the vote. The archive now holds two accounts of why it fails when it fails. Mechanistically, incorrect trajectories converge to their wrong answer earlier than correct ones converge to the right one, so counting votes rewards the failure mode. Formally, its failure probability does not decay to zero at all: a wrong answer holding 46% against a right answer's 45% converges to the wrong one however many samples are drawn, which is the precise reason self-consistency saturates and then degrades, and the reason the tournament aggregations that do have vanishing failure probability are built on pairwise comparison instead. A third entry attacks its cost rather than its accuracy, stopping the sampling by a Bayesian posterior over which answer is the mode and proving that tracking the top two counts is asymptotically enough.

## What we have settled

- **Established** — Sampling more candidates is not monotone in accuracy: extra samples strengthen whatever the aggregation rule already does, including when it is wrong.
  - Two independent demonstrations with different aggregation rules. At inference, best-of-3 selection scores 0.093 against best-of-1's 0.153 on one model and dataset — three samples worse than one — which the authors attribute to a selector that does not exploit the candidate set, so extra samples add plausible wrong answers. In label-free RLVR training, where the reward is the consensus answer among rollouts, 64 rollouts reach 34.42 average accuracy against 34.66 at 16 and at 3.6x the wall clock, because more samples make a spurious consensus easier to form around an incorrect answer. The mechanism is shared even though one is a selector and the other a reward: the sample count amplifies the aggregation rule rather than averaging its errors away. The practical consequence is that a sample-count sweep is a required control, not an optional one, and that a scaling curve reported only at its endpoints can hide a reversal in between. A third instance is the cleanest of all, because it holds the candidate bank fixed and varies only the reducer: selecting by mean token log probability falls from 75.56% at one sample to 65.83% at eighty on the same banks where literal answer plurality rises to 78.33%, so nothing but the selection rule can explain the decline.

- [Optimal Bayesian Stopping for Efficient Inference of Consistent LLM Answers](../../archive/papers/2026/local-5c4c22504406a6aa/summary.md) — Stops self-consistency sampling by Bayesian posterior over which answer is the mode, and proves that tracking only the top two answer counts plus an aggregate is enough for asymptotic optimality.
